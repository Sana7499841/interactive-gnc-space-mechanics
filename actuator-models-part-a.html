4.4 Part A — Actuator Fundamentals, Physics, and Models

Part A develops the physical and mathematical foundations required to understand how spacecraft actuators generate force, torque, momentum exchange, or impulse.

The emphasis is on the actuator itself:

\[ \boxed{\text{What is the actuator?}} \]

\[ \boxed{ \text{How does it physically generate control effort?} } \]

\[ \boxed{ \text{How does actuator geometry map into spacecraft force and torque?} }. \]

The detailed control-authority, constraint, uncertainty, failure, and verification questions are developed separately in Part B.

The overall progression in Part A is

\[ \boxed{\text{Actuator Fundamentals}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Actuator Geometry}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Effectiveness Matrix}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Individual Actuator Physics}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Spacecraft Force / Torque Generation}}. \]

4.4.1 Actuator Role in the GNC Loop

Actuators form the physical output stage of the spacecraft GNC loop.

Navigation and estimation determine the spacecraft state, guidance determines the desired state or trajectory, and control determines the effort required to reduce the difference between desired and actual behaviour.

The actuator system must then transform that calculated control effort into physical action.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{\text{Estimated State}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Guidance}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Control Law}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Desired Force / Torque}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Actuators}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Spacecraft Motion}}. \]

An important distinction is

\[ \boxed{ \text{Controller Output} \neq \text{Physical Force / Torque Automatically} }. \]

The controller may request a force, torque, angular momentum change, wheel torque, magnetic dipole, thrust vector, or actuator-specific command.

The actuator hardware must convert this command into a realizable physical effect.

Commanded and Realized Effort

Let

\[ \mathbf u_c \]

represent the commanded actuator effort, while

\[ \mathbf u_a \]

represents the actuator effort actually produced.

Ideally,

\[ \mathbf u_a = \mathbf u_c. \]

Real actuator physics can cause

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf u_a \neq \mathbf u_c }. \]

The difference may arise from dynamics, saturation, timing, mechanical limitations, uncertainty, or hardware constraints.

4.4.2 Force, Torque, Impulse and Momentum Fundamentals

Spacecraft actuators influence motion through force, torque, impulse, momentum, or angular momentum exchange.

Force

Force changes linear momentum:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F = \frac{d\mathbf p}{dt} }. \]

For a spacecraft of constant mass,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F = m\mathbf a }. \]

Torque

Torque changes angular momentum:

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \frac{d\mathbf H}{dt} }. \]

A force acting at a lever arm \(\mathbf r\) relative to the spacecraft center of mass produces

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F }. \]

Linear Impulse

The impulse delivered by a force over a finite time interval is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf J = \int_{t_0}^{t_f} \mathbf F(t)\,dt }. \]

For approximately constant spacecraft mass,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\mathbf v \approx \frac{\mathbf J}{m} }. \]

Angular Impulse

Angular impulse is

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\mathbf H = \int_{t_0}^{t_f} \boldsymbol{\tau}(t)\,dt }. \]

Momentum Exchange

Some spacecraft actuators, such as reaction wheels, do not expel mass. Instead, they exchange angular momentum internally with the spacecraft.

Other actuators, such as thrusters, generate external force by accelerating propellant.

The type of momentum exchange therefore distinguishes important actuator families.

4.4.3 Actuator Coordinate Frames and Geometry

Actuator force or torque must always be expressed in a clearly defined coordinate frame.

Common actuator-modelling frames include

  • spacecraft body frame \(B\),
  • local actuator frame \(A_i\),
  • thruster nozzle or thrust frame,
  • wheel spin-axis frame,
  • CMG gimbal frame.

Suppose actuator \(i\) has a direction

\[ \hat{\mathbf d}_i^{A_i} \]

defined in its own local actuator frame.

The corresponding body-frame direction is

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf d}_i^B = \mathbf C_{A_i}^{B} \hat{\mathbf d}_i^{A_i} }. \]

Here,

\[ \mathbf C_{A_i}^{B} \]

is the direction-cosine matrix mapping actuator-frame coordinates into spacecraft body-frame coordinates.

Mounting Position

Let

\[ \mathbf r_i^B \]

denote the actuator location relative to the spacecraft center of mass.

A force produced by that actuator therefore generates

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_i^B = \mathbf r_i^B \times \mathbf F_i^B }. \]

Actuator geometry therefore determines whether an actuator produces primarily translation, primarily rotation, or a coupled combination.

Importance of the Center of Mass

The lever arm is measured from the spacecraft center of mass.

If the center of mass changes because of fuel depletion or moving mechanisms, the actuator torque may also change:

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf r_i(t) \times \mathbf F_i }. \]

4.4.4 Actuator Effectiveness Matrix

The actuator effectiveness matrix provides a compact representation of how individual actuator commands combine to produce spacecraft force and torque.

Define the generalized spacecraft wrench

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf w = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf F\\ \boldsymbol{\tau} \end{bmatrix} }. \]

In component form,

\[ \mathbf w = \begin{bmatrix} F_x\\ F_y\\ F_z\\ \tau_x\\ \tau_y\\ \tau_z \end{bmatrix}. \]

For an actuator-command vector

\[ \mathbf u = \begin{bmatrix} u_1\\ u_2\\ \vdots\\ u_n \end{bmatrix}, \]

the generalized wrench may be written as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf w = \mathbf B_a \mathbf u }. \]

Here,

  • \(\mathbf B_a\) is the actuator effectiveness matrix,
  • each column represents the effect of one actuator,
  • the upper rows represent force contribution,
  • the lower rows represent torque contribution.

Single Thruster Column

For a single thruster \(i\),

\[ \mathbf F_i = F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i \]

and

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf r_i \times F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i. \]

Its effectiveness column can therefore be written conceptually as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf b_i = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\mathbf d}_i\\ \mathbf r_i\times\hat{\mathbf d}_i \end{bmatrix} }. \]

If command \(u_i\) scales the available thrust magnitude, then

\[ \mathbf w_i = \mathbf b_i u_i. \]

Collecting all actuator columns gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf B_a = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf b_1 & \mathbf b_2 & \cdots & \mathbf b_n \end{bmatrix} }. \]

This matrix later becomes the foundation for control-authority analysis and control allocation.

Reaction Wheels

Reaction wheels provide spacecraft attitude control by exchanging angular momentum between a rotating wheel and the spacecraft body.

Unlike thrusters, reaction wheels do not normally produce translational force.

Their main role is precise rotational control.

4.4.5 Reaction Wheel Fundamentals

A reaction wheel consists primarily of a motor-driven rotor mounted inside the spacecraft.

When the wheel accelerates in one rotational direction, conservation of angular momentum causes the spacecraft body to experience an approximately equal and opposite angular momentum change.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{\text{Wheel Accelerates}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Wheel Angular Momentum Changes}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Spacecraft Reacts in Opposite Direction}}. \]

Reaction wheels are commonly used for

  • fine attitude pointing,
  • slew manoeuvres,
  • disturbance rejection,
  • payload pointing,
  • three-axis attitude stabilization.

4.4.6 Reaction Wheel Torque and Momentum

For a wheel rotating about a fixed unit spin axis \(\hat{\mathbf s}\), the wheel angular momentum is approximately

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf H_w = I_w\Omega_w\hat{\mathbf s} }. \]

Here,

  • \(I_w\) is wheel rotational inertia,
  • \(\Omega_w\) is wheel angular speed.

For a fixed wheel axis,

\[ \dot{\mathbf H}_w = I_w\dot{\Omega}_w\hat{\mathbf s}. \]

Conservation of angular momentum gives approximately

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{body} = -\dot{\mathbf H}_w }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{body} = -I_w\dot{\Omega}_w\hat{\mathbf s} }. \]

4.4.7 Wheel Speed Dynamics

The wheel motor applies torque to change wheel speed.

A simplified wheel equation is

\[ \boxed{ I_w\dot{\Omega}_w = \tau_m - \tau_f }. \]

Here,

  • \(\tau_m\) is motor torque,
  • \(\tau_f\) represents friction and losses.

For an ideal wheel,

\[ I_w\dot{\Omega}_w = \tau_m. \]

The resulting spacecraft-body torque is approximately opposite to the motor torque applied to the wheel.

4.4.8 Momentum Storage and Saturation

Reaction wheels can store only finite angular momentum.

If the wheel-speed limit is

\[ |\Omega_w| \leq \Omega_{w,\max}, \]

then the corresponding approximate momentum-storage limit is

\[ \boxed{ |H_w| \leq H_{w,\max} = I_w\Omega_{w,\max} }. \]

As

\[ |H_w| \rightarrow H_{w,\max}, \]

the wheel approaches saturation.

Once saturated, it may no longer be able to generate the commanded torque in the required direction.

4.4.9 Wheel Friction and Non-Idealities

Real reaction wheels are affected by mechanical and electrical non-idealities.

Representative effects include

  • bearing friction,
  • viscous friction,
  • Coulomb friction,
  • motor torque ripple,
  • electronic commutation effects,
  • wheel imbalance,
  • structural vibration,
  • speed-dependent disturbances,
  • deadband near zero speed.

A simple friction model may use

\[ \boxed{ \tau_f = b\Omega_w + \tau_c\,\operatorname{sgn}(\Omega_w) }. \]

Here,

  • \(b\) represents viscous friction,
  • \(\tau_c\) represents Coulomb friction.

4.4.10 Reaction Wheel Assemblies

A single reaction wheel produces torque primarily along one spin axis.

Three-axis attitude control therefore requires multiple wheels with independent geometry.

Common arrangements include

  • three orthogonal wheels,
  • four-wheel pyramidal configurations,
  • larger redundant wheel clusters.

For \(n\) reaction wheels, define the wheel-axis matrix

\[ \mathbf A_w = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\mathbf s}_1 & \hat{\mathbf s}_2 & \cdots & \hat{\mathbf s}_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

If the wheel torque vector is

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_w = \begin{bmatrix} \tau_{w1}\\ \tau_{w2}\\ \vdots\\ \tau_{wn} \end{bmatrix}, \]

then the spacecraft torque can be represented as

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{body} = -\mathbf A_w \boldsymbol{\tau}_w }. \]

4.4.11 Wheel Geometry and Torque Authority

Reaction-wheel torque authority depends on wheel orientation and available motor torque.

For the wheel-axis matrix

\[ \mathbf A_w, \]

the spacecraft torque is generated through

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{body} = -\mathbf A_w\boldsymbol{\tau}_w. \]

Wheel geometry determines

  • which torque directions are achievable,
  • how strongly each body axis can be actuated,
  • how redundancy can be used,
  • how the system behaves after wheel failure.

A four-wheel pyramid is commonly used because it provides redundancy while maintaining three-axis torque capability.

4.4.12 Momentum Dumping Requirements

Environmental disturbance torques may cause reaction wheels to accumulate angular momentum over time.

Typical disturbances include

  • gravity-gradient torque,
  • aerodynamic torque,
  • solar-radiation-pressure torque,
  • magnetic disturbance torque,
  • internal mechanism disturbances.

Continued momentum accumulation may drive wheel speeds toward saturation.

Momentum dumping uses an external torque source to remove stored angular momentum from the wheel system.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{\text{Wheel Momentum Builds}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{External Desaturation Torque}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Wheel Speed Reduced}}. \]

Magnetorquers or thrusters are commonly used for momentum unloading.

Control-Moment Gyroscopes

Control-moment gyroscopes generate torque by rotating the direction of a stored angular-momentum vector.

They can produce higher torque than many conventional reaction-wheel systems, but their geometry introduces steering and singularity considerations.

4.4.13 Control Moment Gyroscope Fundamentals

A control-moment gyroscope contains a spinning rotor with angular momentum

\[ \mathbf H = H\hat{\mathbf h}. \]

Unlike a reaction wheel, which primarily changes the magnitude of wheel angular momentum, a CMG generates torque mainly by changing the direction of the angular-momentum vector.

A gimbal rotates the wheel spin axis, causing

\[ \dot{\mathbf H} \neq \mathbf 0. \]

The spacecraft experiences the reaction torque associated with that momentum-vector change.

4.4.14 CMG Torque Generation

The torque generated by a CMG is related to the time rate of change of its angular momentum.

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = -\dot{\mathbf H} }. \]

For approximately constant momentum magnitude \(H\),

\[ \dot{\mathbf H} = \boldsymbol{\omega}_g \times \mathbf H, \]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\omega}_g \]

is the gimbal angular-velocity vector.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = -\boldsymbol{\omega}_g \times \mathbf H }. \]

4.4.15 CMG Steering Geometry

A multi-CMG spacecraft must determine how gimbal rates should be chosen to generate a desired spacecraft torque.

A general relation can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = -\mathbf A_{CMG}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) \dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}} }. \]

Here,

  • \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) contains the CMG gimbal angles,
  • \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\) contains the gimbal rates,
  • \(\mathbf A_{CMG}\) is the configuration-dependent Jacobian.

The matrix changes continuously as the gimbal angles change.

4.4.16 CMG Singularities

A CMG configuration becomes singular when the gimbal geometry loses the ability to generate torque in one or more required directions.

Mathematically, this occurs when the steering matrix loses effective rank or becomes poorly conditioned.

Near a singularity,

  • very large gimbal rates may be required,
  • some torque directions may become weak,
  • the steering law may become numerically sensitive.

Singularity management is therefore a central CMG-control problem.

4.4.17 CMG Control Authority

CMG control authority depends on

  • stored wheel angular momentum,
  • gimbal-rate limits,
  • number of CMGs,
  • cluster geometry,
  • current gimbal angles,
  • proximity to singular configurations.

The available torque is therefore time-varying and configuration dependent.

This is fundamentally different from an ideal actuator model with fixed axis-independent torque capability.

Magnetorquers

Magnetorquers generate spacecraft torque by interacting with the Earth's magnetic field.

They require no propellant and are widely used on small spacecraft, particularly for detumbling, coarse attitude control, and momentum unloading.

4.4.18 Magnetorquer Fundamentals

A magnetorquer generates a commanded magnetic dipole moment

\[ \mathbf m. \]

This dipole interacts with the local geomagnetic field

\[ \mathbf B \]

to create a spacecraft torque.

Common magnetorquer hardware includes

  • magnetic torque rods,
  • air-core coils,
  • printed-circuit-board coils.

4.4.19 Magnetic Dipole Generation

For a simple current-carrying coil,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf m = NIA\hat{\mathbf n} }. \]

Here,

  • \(N\) is the number of turns,
  • \(I\) is the current,
  • \(A\) is coil area,
  • \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) is the coil normal direction.

For a three-axis system,

\[ \mathbf m = \begin{bmatrix} m_x\\ m_y\\ m_z \end{bmatrix}. \]

4.4.20 Magnetic Torque Equation

The fundamental magnetorquer torque equation is

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf m \times \mathbf B }. \]

The torque magnitude is

\[ \boxed{ \tau = mB\sin\theta }. \]

Here,

\[ \theta \]

is the angle between the commanded dipole vector and local magnetic field.

4.4.21 Geomagnetic Field Dependence

Magnetorquer authority depends directly on the local geomagnetic-field vector.

Because

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf m \times \mathbf B, \]

both the magnitude and direction of available torque vary as the spacecraft moves through its orbit.

The geomagnetic field depends on

  • orbital position,
  • altitude,
  • inclination,
  • Earth magnetic-field geometry,
  • time-varying magnetic conditions.

4.4.22 Magnetorquer Authority and Limitations

Magnetorquers possess a fundamental instantaneous limitation.

Since

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf m \times \mathbf B, \]

the generated torque is always perpendicular to the magnetic-field vector.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\mathbf B = 0 }. \]

A magnetorquer cannot generate instantaneous torque parallel to \(\mathbf B\).

Over time, however, the orbital variation of the field direction may provide effective three-axis control.

4.4.23 Momentum Dumping with Magnetorquers

Magnetorquers are frequently used to remove stored angular momentum from reaction-wheel systems.

The wheel system generates an internal torque, while the magnetorquer generates an external torque against Earth's magnetic field.

This allows wheel speed to be reduced without significantly disturbing the desired spacecraft attitude.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{\text{Reaction Wheel Momentum}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Magnetic Desaturation Torque}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Reduced Wheel Speed}}. \]

Thrusters

Thrusters are among the most versatile spacecraft actuators because they generate external force and can therefore influence both translation and rotation.

Thruster modelling requires not only the thrust magnitude, but also geometry, direction, timing, propellant behaviour, valve dynamics, and firing constraints.

4.4.24 Thruster Fundamentals

A spacecraft thruster generates force by accelerating propellant and expelling it from the vehicle.

The spacecraft experiences a reaction force in the opposite direction.

A generic thruster may therefore be characterised by

  • thrust magnitude,
  • thrust direction,
  • mounting location,
  • specific impulse,
  • minimum impulse bit,
  • minimum on-time,
  • valve dynamics,
  • thrust rise and fall time.

4.4.25 Thrust Force Model

For thruster \(i\), let

\[ F_i \]

denote the thrust magnitude and

\[ \hat{\mathbf d}_i \]

the unit thrust direction.

The thrust-force vector is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F_i = F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i }. \]

For \(n\) simultaneously active thrusters,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F_{net} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf F_i }. \]

4.4.26 Thruster Torque Model

A thruster located away from the spacecraft center of mass generates both force and torque.

Let

\[ \mathbf r_i \]

be the position vector from the center of mass to thruster \(i\).

Then

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf r_i \times F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i }. \]

The total thruster-generated torque is

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{net} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \boldsymbol{\tau}_i }. \]

4.4.27 Thruster Position and Direction Vectors

Two geometric quantities are essential for each thruster:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf r_i } \qquad \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf d}_i }. \]

The position vector determines the lever arm relative to the center of mass.

The direction vector determines the direction in which force acts.

Together they define the complete first-order wrench contribution of the thruster.

For each thruster,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf w_i = \begin{bmatrix} F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i\\ \mathbf r_i\times F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i \end{bmatrix} }. \]

4.4.28 Thruster Cant Angles

Thrusters are not always aligned exactly with a principal spacecraft body axis.

A thruster may be intentionally mounted at a cant angle to provide force and torque components in multiple directions.

For example, a planar cant angle \(\alpha\) may produce

\[ F_x = F\cos\alpha, \]

\[ F_y = F\sin\alpha. \]

In three dimensions, the actual thrust direction is defined using rotation matrices or direction cosines.

Canting affects

  • available translational authority,
  • available rotational authority,
  • cross-axis coupling,
  • allocator performance,
  • propellant efficiency.

4.4.29 Specific Impulse and Propellant Consumption

Specific impulse is a common measure of propulsion efficiency.

It is defined by

\[ \boxed{ I_{sp} = \frac{F} {\dot m g_0} }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \dot m = \frac{F} {I_{sp}g_0} }. \]

Here,

  • \(\dot m\) is propellant mass-flow rate,
  • \(g_0\) is standard gravitational acceleration.

During a burn,

\[ \boxed{ m_p = \int \dot m\,dt }. \]

Propellant consumption therefore connects actuator modelling with mass depletion and long-term mission capability.

4.4.30 Minimum Impulse Bit

Pulsed thrusters cannot necessarily produce arbitrarily small impulse.

The smallest repeatable impulse that can be commanded is known as the minimum impulse bit.

\[ \boxed{ J_{MIB} = \min \left( \int F(t)\,dt \right) }. \]

For approximately constant thrust,

\[ J \approx Ft_{on}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ J_{MIB} \approx F t_{\min} } \]

for an ideal rectangular pulse.

MIB becomes especially important during precision rendezvous, docking, fine translation, and small attitude corrections.

4.4.31 Minimum On-Time and Valve Dynamics

A thruster requires finite time for its valve and flow system to respond.

The commanded on-time must therefore normally satisfy

\[ \boxed{ t_{on} \geq t_{\min} }. \]

If

\[ t_{on} < t_{\min}, \]

the commanded pulse may not produce a predictable or repeatable impulse.

Valve dynamics may include

  • electrical command delay,
  • valve opening delay,
  • fluid filling dynamics,
  • closing delay,
  • residual thrust after command termination.

4.4.32 Rise Time and Fall Time

Real thrust does not generally jump instantaneously from zero to its steady-state value.

A more realistic thrust history is

\[ 0 \rightarrow \text{Rise} \rightarrow F_{ss} \rightarrow \text{Fall} \rightarrow 0. \]

The delivered impulse is therefore

\[ \boxed{ J = \int F(t)\,dt } \]

rather than simply

\[ Ft_{on} \]

when transient effects are important.

4.4.33 Thrust Magnitude Error

Actual thrust may differ from nominal thrust.

A simple scale-factor representation is

\[ \boxed{ F_{actual} = F_{nom} \left( 1+\epsilon_F \right) }. \]

Alternatively,

\[ \boxed{ F_{actual} = F_{cmd} + \Delta F }. \]

Thrust error may result from

  • pressure variation,
  • temperature variation,
  • manufacturing tolerance,
  • valve variation,
  • propellant-feed behaviour,
  • aging or degradation.

4.4.34 Thruster Misalignment

The actual thrust direction may differ from the nominal design direction.

If

\[ \hat{\mathbf d}_{nom} \]

is the nominal direction, then

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf d}_{actual} \neq \hat{\mathbf d}_{nom} }. \]

A small misalignment may be represented using a small-angle rotation:

\[ \hat{\mathbf d}_{actual} \approx \left( \mathbf I + [\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}]_\times \right) \hat{\mathbf d}_{nom}. \]

Even a small angular error can introduce unwanted cross-axis force and torque.

4.4.35 Valve Delay and Timing Uncertainty

A commanded thruster firing may not begin or end at exactly the commanded time.

The actual ignition time may be represented as

\[ \boxed{ t_{on,actual} = t_{on,cmd} + \Delta t_{open} }. \]

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{ t_{off,actual} = t_{off,cmd} + \Delta t_{close} }. \]

Timing error changes delivered impulse and may become significant when commanded pulses are short.

4.4.36 Plume and Firing Constraints

A thruster may be physically capable of firing but operationally prohibited under certain spacecraft conditions.

Restrictions may arise from

  • plume impingement on solar arrays,
  • plume impingement on sensors,
  • contamination-sensitive payloads,
  • thermal loading,
  • interaction with docking hardware,
  • target-spacecraft safety during RPO,
  • structural or mission constraints.

Therefore the available thruster set may depend on mission phase and spacecraft configuration.

4.4.37 Simultaneous Thruster Firings

Multiple thrusters may fire simultaneously to generate a desired net force or torque.

For active set

\[ \mathcal A, \]

the total force is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F_{net} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal A} \mathbf F_i }. \]

The total torque is

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{net} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal A} \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i }. \]

Simultaneous firing may be used to

  • increase net force,
  • cancel unwanted torque,
  • generate pure torque,
  • generate approximately pure translation,
  • provide redundancy.

4.4.38 Translational Control Authority

Translational control authority describes the force and acceleration capability available from the thruster system.

The net force is

\[ \mathbf F_{net} = \sum_i \mathbf F_i. \]

The corresponding translational acceleration is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf a = \frac{\mathbf F_{net}}{m} }. \]

Therefore spacecraft mass directly affects acceleration authority.

As propellant is consumed and spacecraft mass decreases, the same thrust may produce greater acceleration.

4.4.39 Rotational Control Authority

Rotational thruster authority is determined by the net torque generated around the spacecraft center of mass.

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{net} = \sum_i \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i }. \]

For rigid-body rotational dynamics,

\[ \mathbf I\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf I\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{net} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{dist}. \]

Thruster torque authority therefore depends on

  • thrust magnitude,
  • lever arm,
  • thruster direction,
  • spacecraft inertia,
  • which thrusters can fire simultaneously.

4.4.40 Thruster Configuration Matrices

The complete thruster system can be represented using a configuration or effectiveness matrix.

For \(n\) thrusters,

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf F\\ \boldsymbol{\tau} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf B_T \mathbf u_T }. \]

Define

\[ \mathbf B_T = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\mathbf d}_1 & \hat{\mathbf d}_2 & \cdots & \hat{\mathbf d}_n\\ \mathbf r_1\times\hat{\mathbf d}_1 & \mathbf r_2\times\hat{\mathbf d}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf r_n\times\hat{\mathbf d}_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

If each command \(u_i\) represents the corresponding thrust magnitude, this matrix maps the individual thruster efforts into the complete spacecraft wrench.

This equation is the mathematical bridge between thruster geometry and later control-allocation algorithms.

Other Actuator Technologies

The same force, torque, geometry, command, and effectiveness concepts can be applied to other actuator technologies used in spacecraft and atmospheric vehicles.

4.4.41 Cold-Gas Thrusters

Cold-gas thrusters generate thrust by releasing pressurized gas through a nozzle without combustion.

Their main characteristics include

  • simple architecture,
  • relatively low thermal complexity,
  • clean exhaust,
  • relatively modest specific impulse,
  • suitability for fine translation and attitude control.

The same basic thrust relation applies:

\[ \mathbf F_i = F_i\hat{\mathbf d}_i. \]

4.4.42 Chemical Thrusters

Chemical thrusters generate thrust through energetic chemical reactions or decomposition processes.

They are widely used where relatively high thrust or impulsive manoeuvring capability is required.

Important modelling quantities include

  • thrust magnitude,
  • specific impulse,
  • mass flow,
  • chamber-pressure dependence,
  • valve dynamics,
  • thrust transients,
  • minimum impulse bit,
  • thermal and duty-cycle constraints.

4.4.43 Electric Propulsion Actuators

Electric propulsion systems accelerate propellant using electrical or electromagnetic processes.

They commonly provide

  • high specific impulse,
  • low thrust,
  • long-duration operation,
  • high electrical-power dependence.

Electric propulsion is therefore especially suitable for applications such as

  • orbit raising,
  • station keeping,
  • deep-space propulsion,
  • long-duration trajectory shaping.

Its actuator model may require electrical power, throttle level, efficiency, thrust magnitude, and mass-flow relationships.

4.4.44 Gimballed Thrusters

A gimballed thruster changes the direction of its thrust vector by rotating the engine or nozzle.

If the nominal thrust magnitude is \(F\), then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F = F\hat{\mathbf d}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) } \]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\delta} \]

represents gimbal angles.

The torque is

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf r \times F\hat{\mathbf d}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) }. \]

Gimbal angle limits and gimbal-rate limits constrain the available thrust-vector direction.

4.4.45 Control Surfaces for Atmospheric Vehicles

Atmospheric vehicles can generate control forces and moments by deflecting aerodynamic surfaces.

Examples include

  • ailerons,
  • elevators,
  • rudders,
  • elevons,
  • canards,
  • flaps.

Aerodynamic control effectiveness depends strongly on dynamic pressure.

\[ \boxed{ q = \frac12\rho V^2 }. \]

A control-surface moment may be represented approximately as

\[ \boxed{ M = qSC_m(\delta) }. \]

Therefore control authority varies strongly with air density, velocity, angle of attack, and control-surface deflection.

4.4.46 Thrust-Vector-Control Systems

Thrust-vector control changes the direction of propulsion force to generate attitude-control moments.

It is commonly used in

  • launch vehicles,
  • upper stages,
  • missiles,
  • powered descent vehicles,
  • large propulsion systems.

For thrust magnitude \(T\) and direction \(\hat{\mathbf d}\),

\[ \mathbf F = T\hat{\mathbf d}. \]

The corresponding torque is

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf r \times T\hat{\mathbf d} }. \]

TVC effectiveness depends on

  • thrust magnitude,
  • lever arm,
  • gimbal angle,
  • gimbal rate,
  • propulsion operating condition.
