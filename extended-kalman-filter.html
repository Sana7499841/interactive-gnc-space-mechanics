Kalman Filter Fundamentals

The Kalman Filter (KF) is one of the most important recursive state-estimation algorithms used in spacecraft Guidance, Navigation and Control.

It combines two imperfect sources of information:

\[ \boxed{\text{Mathematical Model of the Spacecraft}} + \boxed{\text{Noisy Sensor Measurements}} \]

to estimate the spacecraft state.

The Bayesian-estimation framework developed previously introduced the general probabilistic principle

\[ \boxed{ \text{Prior} + \text{Measurement Information} \rightarrow \text{Posterior} }. \]

The Kalman Filter turns this principle into an efficient recursive algorithm for linear stochastic systems.

At every estimation cycle, two fundamental operations are performed:

\[ \boxed{\text{Prediction}} \]

followed by

\[ \boxed{\text{Measurement Update}}. \]

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Previous Estimate} \rightarrow \text{Predict} \rightarrow \text{Compare with Measurement} \rightarrow \text{Correct} \rightarrow \text{New Estimate} }. \]

An important feature of Kalman filtering is that the filter does not estimate only the spacecraft state.

It simultaneously estimates how uncertain that state estimate is.

Its two fundamental outputs are therefore

\[ \boxed{\hat{\mathbf x}_k} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\mathbf P_k}. \]

Here, \(\hat{\mathbf x}_k\) is the estimated state and \(\mathbf P_k\) is the state-estimation-error covariance.

This distinction is particularly important during operations such as rendezvous, proximity operations and docking.

Knowing that the estimated relative position is 10 cm is not enough. The GNC system also needs to know whether the associated uncertainty is approximately 1 mm, 1 cm, or 1 m.

1. Why the Kalman Filter Is Needed

A spacecraft navigation system generally has two imperfect sources of information.

Spacecraft Dynamics Model

If the present state is known, equations of motion can predict the spacecraft state at the next time.

For example,

\[ \mathbf x_{k+1} = f(\mathbf x_k,\mathbf u_k). \]

However, the mathematical model is never completely perfect.

Sources of model uncertainty may include:

  • uncertain disturbance forces,
  • uncertain disturbance torques,
  • imperfect gravity models,
  • aerodynamic uncertainty,
  • solar-radiation-pressure uncertainty,
  • actuator uncertainty,
  • thruster-magnitude error,
  • thruster misalignment,
  • unmodelled structural dynamics,
  • sensor-bias evolution,
  • numerical approximation.

Sensor Measurements

Sensors observe the real physical spacecraft but their measurements are also imperfect.

Sensor measurements may contain:

  • random noise,
  • bias,
  • quantization,
  • latency,
  • scale-factor error,
  • alignment error,
  • environmental disturbances,
  • occasional outliers.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Dynamics Model} = \text{Continuous but Imperfect Prediction} } \]

while

\[ \boxed{ \text{Sensor} = \text{Physical Observation but Noisy Measurement} }. \]

The Kalman Filter combines the strengths of both sources.

2. Fundamental Idea of Kalman Filtering

Suppose the dynamic model predicts

\[ x = 100~\text{m} \]

while a sensor reports

\[ z = 103~\text{m}. \]

Which value should the navigation system use?

The answer depends on uncertainty.

If the prediction is highly reliable and the sensor is noisy, the corrected estimate should remain closer to 100 m.

If the model prediction is uncertain while the sensor is highly accurate, the corrected estimate should move closer to 103 m.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Correction Strength} = f( \text{Prediction Uncertainty}, \text{Measurement Uncertainty} ) }. \]

The Kalman Filter calculates this weighting mathematically through the Kalman gain.

Kalman filtering is therefore not simple averaging:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Filtering} \neq \frac{\text{Prediction}+\text{Measurement}}{2} }. \]

Instead,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Filtering} = \text{Uncertainty-Weighted State Estimation} }. \]

3. The State Vector

Before constructing the filter, the quantities to be estimated must be defined.

For a simple one-dimensional translational problem,

\[ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ v \end{bmatrix}. \]

For three-dimensional spacecraft navigation,

\[ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v \end{bmatrix}. \]

A more complete state may contain

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v\\ \boldsymbol\theta\\ \boldsymbol\omega\\ \mathbf b_g\\ \mathbf b_a \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Possible estimated quantities include:

  • position,
  • velocity,
  • attitude,
  • angular rate,
  • gyroscope bias,
  • accelerometer bias,
  • GNSS clock bias,
  • GNSS clock drift,
  • sensor alignment errors,
  • disturbance parameters,
  • relative position,
  • relative velocity,
  • relative attitude.

A critical principle is

\[ \boxed{ \text{A State Does Not Need to Be Directly Measured to Be Estimated} }. \]

However, the state must be sufficiently observable through the dynamics and available measurements.

4. Linear Discrete-Time State-Space Model

The classical discrete Kalman Filter assumes a linear process model:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x_k = \mathbf F_{k-1}\mathbf x_{k-1} + \mathbf B_{k-1}\mathbf u_{k-1} + \mathbf w_{k-1} }. \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf x_k\) is the state at time \(k\),
  • \(\mathbf F_{k-1}\) is the state-transition matrix,
  • \(\mathbf u_{k-1}\) is the known control input,
  • \(\mathbf B_{k-1}\) maps the control input into the state,
  • \(\mathbf w_{k-1}\) represents process uncertainty.

Process noise is commonly represented as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf w_{k-1} \sim \mathcal N( \mathbf 0, \mathbf Q_{k-1} ) }. \]

The matrix \(\mathbf Q\) is the process-noise covariance.

5. Physical Meaning of the State-Transition Matrix \(F\)

The matrix

\[ \mathbf F \]

describes how the state evolves from one discrete time step to the next.

Consider a constant-velocity model:

\[ x_{k+1} = x_k+v_k\Delta t \]

and

\[ v_{k+1} = v_k. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_{k+1}\\ v_{k+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_k\\ v_k \end{bmatrix}. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The term \(\Delta t\) couples velocity into future position.

This means velocity uncertainty can also influence future position uncertainty.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Dynamics Propagate Both the State and Relationships Between State Errors} }. \]

6. Control-Input Matrix \(B\)

Known control inputs can be incorporated using

\[ \mathbf B\mathbf u. \]

For constant acceleration \(a\),

\[ x_{k+1} = x_k + v_k\Delta t + \frac12a_k\Delta t^2 \]

and

\[ v_{k+1} = v_k+a_k\Delta t. \]

If

\[ u_k=a_k, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf B = \begin{bmatrix} \frac12\Delta t^2\\ \Delta t \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Spacecraft control inputs may represent:

  • known commanded acceleration,
  • thruster force,
  • reaction-wheel torque,
  • known control torque,
  • processed inertial measurements.

7. Measurement Model

The linear sensor model is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf z_k = \mathbf H_k\mathbf x_k + \mathbf v_k }. \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf z_k\) is the sensor measurement vector,
  • \(\mathbf H_k\) is the measurement matrix,
  • \(\mathbf v_k\) is measurement noise.

The measurement noise is commonly represented as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf v_k \sim \mathcal N( \mathbf 0, \mathbf R_k ) }. \]

The matrix

\[ \boxed{\mathbf R_k} \]

is the measurement-noise covariance.

8. Physical Meaning of the Measurement Matrix \(H\)

The matrix

\[ \mathbf H \]

maps the navigation state into the quantity observed by a sensor.

Suppose

\[ \mathbf x= \begin{bmatrix} x\\ v \end{bmatrix}. \]

Position Measurement Only

If the sensor measures position,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf H = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Velocity Measurement Only

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf H = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Position and Velocity

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf H = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Therefore, \(H\) tells the estimator:

Which combination of the state variables should this sensor observe?

9. Prior and Posterior State Estimates

The Kalman Filter maintains two estimates at each measurement epoch.

Prior Estimate

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} } \]

represents knowledge before processing the current measurement.

Posterior Estimate

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{+} } \]

represents knowledge after the measurement update.

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{\mathbf P_k^{-}} \]

is the predicted covariance and

\[ \boxed{\mathbf P_k^{+}} \]

is the posterior covariance.

The complete cycle is

\[ \boxed{ ( \hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1}^{+}, \mathbf P_{k-1}^{+} ) } \]

\[ \downarrow \]

Prediction

\[ \downarrow \]

\[ \boxed{ ( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-}, \mathbf P_k^{-} ) } \]

\[ \downarrow \]

Measurement Update

\[ \downarrow \]

\[ \boxed{ ( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{+}, \mathbf P_k^{+} ) }. \]

10. Step 1 — State Prediction

The predicted state is

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} = \mathbf F_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1}^{+} + \mathbf B_{k-1}\mathbf u_{k-1} }. \]

This equation asks:

Based on the previous best estimate and the spacecraft dynamic model, where should the spacecraft state be now?

No new measurement has yet been processed.

This prediction may propagate:

  • orbit position and velocity,
  • relative RPO state,
  • inertial-navigation states,
  • attitude error states,
  • sensor biases.

11. Step 2 — Covariance Prediction

State prediction must be accompanied by uncertainty prediction:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P_k^{-} = \mathbf F_{k-1} \mathbf P_{k-1}^{+} \mathbf F_{k-1}^{T} + \mathbf Q_{k-1} }. \]

The term

\[ \mathbf F \mathbf P^{+} \mathbf F^T \]

propagates existing uncertainty through the dynamics.

The term

\[ \mathbf Q \]

adds new uncertainty caused by imperfect process modelling.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Predicted Uncertainty} = \text{Propagated Previous Uncertainty} + \text{New Model Uncertainty} }. \]

12. Why Covariance Can Grow During Propagation

During a sensor outage, the estimator can continue propagating the spacecraft state.

However, uncertain disturbances and model imperfections continue to accumulate.

Thus the state estimate still exists, but confidence in that estimate generally decreases.

Mathematically,

\[ \mathbf P^{-} \]

normally grows.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{No Measurement} \not\Rightarrow \text{No Estimate} } \]

but normally

\[ \boxed{ \text{No Measurement} \Rightarrow \text{Increasing Uncertainty} }. \]

13. Understanding Process Noise \(Q\)

The matrix

\[ \boxed{\mathbf Q} \]

describes uncertainty in state evolution.

It can represent:

  • unmodelled acceleration,
  • disturbance-force uncertainty,
  • disturbance-torque uncertainty,
  • atmospheric-drag uncertainty,
  • solar-radiation-pressure uncertainty,
  • thruster-force uncertainty,
  • actuator errors,
  • sensor-bias random walks,
  • model truncation,
  • neglected dynamics.

A very small \(Q\) tells the filter:

Trust the model strongly.

A larger \(Q\) says:

The model prediction may be inaccurate.

14. \(Q\) Is Not Simply a Tuning Knob

Although \(Q\) is often adjusted during filter tuning, it should ideally be derived from a physical stochastic model.

For example, if acceleration uncertainty is represented as continuous white acceleration noise with spectral density \(q_a\), the constant-velocity model can lead to

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf Q = q_a \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\Delta t^3}{3} & \frac{\Delta t^2}{2} \\[6pt] \frac{\Delta t^2}{2} & \Delta t \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Different process-noise models can produce different discrete \(Q\) matrices.

Therefore a good workflow is

\[ \boxed{ \text{Physical Disturbance Model} \rightarrow \text{Continuous Stochastic Model} \rightarrow \text{Discretization} \rightarrow Q_d }. \]

15. Measurement Prediction

Once the state has been predicted, the filter calculates the measurement expected from that predicted state.

For the linear case,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf z}_k = \mathbf H_k \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} }. \]

This answers:

If the predicted spacecraft state were correct, what should the sensor measure?

16. Innovation or Measurement Residual

The difference between the actual measurement and predicted measurement is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf z_k - \mathbf H_k \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} }. \]

The vector \(\mathbf y_k\) is called the:

  • innovation,
  • measurement residual,
  • pre-fit residual.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Innovation} = \text{Actual Measurement} - \text{Expected Measurement} }. \]

If

\[ \mathbf y_k\approx0, \]

the sensor agrees with the prediction.

A large innovation means that prediction and measurement disagree.

However, a large raw residual is not enough to conclude that a sensor is faulty.

Its importance depends on the expected residual uncertainty.

17. Innovation Covariance

The innovation covariance is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf S_k = \mathbf H_k \mathbf P_k^{-} \mathbf H_k^T + \mathbf R_k }. \]

It combines:

\[ \mathbf H\mathbf P^{-}\mathbf H^T \]

which represents predicted-measurement uncertainty due to state uncertainty,

and

\[ \mathbf R \]

which represents sensor uncertainty.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Innovation Uncertainty} = \text{Projected State Uncertainty} + \text{Sensor Uncertainty} }. \]

18. Why Innovation Covariance Matters

Suppose

\[ y=1~\text{m}. \]

If

\[ \sigma_y=0.01~\text{m}, \]

the innovation is extremely unusual.

But if

\[ \sigma_y=10~\text{m}, \]

the same 1 m residual is relatively insignificant.

Therefore residual magnitude should always be interpreted together with its covariance.

A normalized quantity is

\[ \boxed{ \epsilon_k = \mathbf y_k^T \mathbf S_k^{-1} \mathbf y_k }. \]

This forms the basis of NIS and Mahalanobis-distance gating.

19. Kalman Gain

The Kalman gain is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf K_k = \mathbf P_k^{-} \mathbf H_k^T \mathbf S_k^{-1} }. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf K_k = \mathbf P_k^{-} \mathbf H_k^T \left( \mathbf H_k \mathbf P_k^{-} \mathbf H_k^T + \mathbf R_k \right)^{-1} }. \]

The Kalman gain determines how strongly the innovation modifies the predicted state.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Gain} = \text{Optimal Measurement Correction Weight} } \]

under the classical Kalman assumptions.

20. Physical Interpretation of the Kalman Gain

Consider a scalar state measured directly:

\[ H=1. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ K = \frac{P^-}{P^-+R} }. \]

Prediction Much More Uncertain Than the Sensor

If

\[ P^-\gg R, \]

then

\[ K\rightarrow1. \]

The filter responds strongly to the measurement.

Sensor Much More Uncertain Than the Prediction

If

\[ R\gg P^-, \]

then

\[ K\rightarrow0. \]

The filter relies mainly on prediction.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P^- \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Measurement Influence} \uparrow } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ R \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Measurement Influence} \downarrow }. \]

21. Kalman Gain Is Not a Fixed Gain

Unlike a conventional fixed-gain observer,

\[ \boxed{\mathbf K_k} \]

generally varies with time.

As the covariance changes,

\[ \mathbf P_k \]

changes the gain.

If sensor uncertainty changes and \(R_k\) is modified, the gain also changes.

Therefore the Kalman Filter continuously adjusts measurement influence according to the uncertainty model.

22. State Measurement Update

The corrected state is

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{+} = \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} + \mathbf K_k\mathbf y_k }. \]

Substituting the innovation,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{+} = \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} + \mathbf K_k \left( \mathbf z_k - \mathbf H_k\hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} \right) }. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Corrected Estimate} = \text{Predicted Estimate} + \text{Weighted Measurement Disagreement} }. \]

The innovation tells us what correction is required.

The Kalman gain tells us how much correction should be applied.

23. Posterior Covariance Update

After incorporating a measurement, the state covariance must also be updated.

A common expression is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P_k^{+} = ( \mathbf I-\mathbf K_k\mathbf H_k ) \mathbf P_k^{-} }. \]

An informative valid measurement usually reduces state uncertainty:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P_k^{+} \preceq \mathbf P_k^{-} }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Valid Informative Measurement} \rightarrow \text{Reduced Uncertainty} }. \]

24. Joseph-Form Covariance Update

A numerically robust covariance update is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P_k^{+} = ( \mathbf I-\mathbf K_k\mathbf H_k ) \mathbf P_k^{-} ( \mathbf I-\mathbf K_k\mathbf H_k )^T + \mathbf K_k \mathbf R_k \mathbf K_k^T }. \]

This is known as the Joseph form.

In exact arithmetic it is equivalent to the simplified covariance update when \(K\) is the Kalman gain.

In finite-precision implementation it can help preserve:

  • covariance symmetry,
  • positive-semidefinite behaviour,
  • numerical robustness.

25. Complete Kalman Filter Cycle

Prediction — State

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} = \mathbf F_{k-1} \hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1}^{+} + \mathbf B_{k-1} \mathbf u_{k-1} } \]

Prediction — Covariance

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P_k^{-} = \mathbf F_{k-1} \mathbf P_{k-1}^{+} \mathbf F_{k-1}^{T} + \mathbf Q_{k-1} } \]

Predicted Measurement

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf z}_k = \mathbf H_k \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} } \]

Innovation

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf z_k - \hat{\mathbf z}_k } \]

Innovation Covariance

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf S_k = \mathbf H_k \mathbf P_k^{-} \mathbf H_k^T + \mathbf R_k } \]

Kalman Gain

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf K_k = \mathbf P_k^{-} \mathbf H_k^T \mathbf S_k^{-1} } \]

State Update

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{+} = \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} + \mathbf K_k\mathbf y_k } \]

Covariance Update

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P_k^{+} = ( \mathbf I-\mathbf K_k\mathbf H_k ) \mathbf P_k^{-} } \]

The posterior then becomes the starting condition for the next prediction.

26. Complete Information Flow

The state-estimation path is

\[ \boxed{\text{Previous Posterior State}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Dynamics Model}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Predicted State}}. \]

In parallel, uncertainty evolves as

\[ \boxed{\text{Previous Covariance}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Dynamics + Process Noise}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Predicted Covariance}}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{\text{Sensor Measurement}} - \boxed{\text{Predicted Measurement}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Innovation}}. \]

The predicted covariance and measurement covariance determine

\[ \boxed{\text{Innovation Covariance}} \]

which contributes to

\[ \boxed{\text{Kalman Gain}}. \]

Finally,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Prediction} + \text{Kalman Gain} \times \text{Innovation} } \]

gives the corrected state estimate.

27. What \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\), \(S\), and \(K\) Mean

\(P\): State-Estimation Error Covariance

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf P = E \left[ ( \mathbf x-\hat{\mathbf x} ) ( \mathbf x-\hat{\mathbf x} )^T \right] }. \]

It represents uncertainty in the estimated state.

\(Q\): Process-Noise Covariance

\[ \boxed{\mathbf Q} \]

describes uncertainty introduced during state propagation.

\(R\): Measurement-Noise Covariance

\[ \boxed{\mathbf R} \]

describes sensor uncertainty.

\(S\): Innovation Covariance

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf S = \mathbf H\mathbf P^-\mathbf H^T+\mathbf R }. \]

\(K\): Kalman Gain

\[ \boxed{\mathbf K} \]

determines the strength of measurement correction.

A useful conceptual chain is

\[ \boxed{ P,Q,R \rightarrow S \rightarrow K \rightarrow \text{State Correction} }. \]

28. Dimensions of the Main Matrices

Suppose

\[ \mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n, \qquad \mathbf z\in\mathbb R^m, \qquad \mathbf u\in\mathbb R^p. \]

Then:

\[ \mathbf F\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}, \]

\[ \mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}, \]

\[ \mathbf H\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}, \]

\[ \mathbf P,\mathbf Q\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}, \]

\[ \mathbf R,\mathbf S\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}, \]

\[ \mathbf K\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}. \]

Matrix-dimension checking is one of the simplest and most useful debugging tools for a Kalman Filter implementation.

29. Simple Position–Velocity Example

Consider

\[ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ v \end{bmatrix}. \]

For constant velocity,

\[ \mathbf F = \begin{bmatrix} 1&\Delta t\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Suppose only position is measured:

\[ z=x+v_m. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf H= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The filter estimates both position and velocity even though velocity is not measured directly.

The position measurement can correct velocity because position and velocity errors become statistically coupled through the dynamics.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{A Measurement Can Correct States That It Does Not Directly Measure} }. \]

30. Cross-Covariance and Indirect State Correction

Suppose

\[ \mathbf P^- = \begin{bmatrix} P_{xx}&P_{xv}\\ P_{vx}&P_{vv} \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ H= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ K = \frac{1}{P_{xx}+R} \begin{bmatrix} P_{xx}\\ P_{vx} \end{bmatrix}. \]

The velocity correction depends on

\[ \frac{P_{vx}}{P_{xx}+R}. \]

Thus the position measurement can modify the velocity estimate through position–velocity cross-covariance.

31. Kalman Filter as a Bayesian Estimator

Before the measurement,

\[ \boxed{ p( \mathbf x_k | \mathbf z_{1:k-1} ) = \mathcal N( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^-, \mathbf P_k^- ) }. \]

The measurement likelihood is

\[ \boxed{ p( \mathbf z_k | \mathbf x_k ) = \mathcal N( \mathbf H_k\mathbf x_k, \mathbf R_k ) }. \]

After measurement,

\[ \boxed{ p( \mathbf x_k | \mathbf z_{1:k} ) = \mathcal N( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^+, \mathbf P_k^+ ) }. \]

Thus a linear-Gaussian Bayesian posterior can be represented completely using

\[ \boxed{\text{Mean}+\text{Covariance}}. \]

32. In What Sense Is the Kalman Filter Optimal?

The classical Kalman Filter is optimal only under specific modelling assumptions.

Under a correctly specified linear stochastic model with appropriate assumptions, the KF provides a minimum mean-square-error estimate.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{The Kalman Filter Is Optimal Under Its Assumed Linear Stochastic Model} }. \]

It is not correct to say that the Kalman Filter is always optimal under every real-world condition.

33. Core Assumptions of the Classical Kalman Filter

The standard formulation assumes

\[ \mathbf x_k = \mathbf F\mathbf x_{k-1} + \mathbf B\mathbf u + \mathbf w \]

and

\[ \mathbf z_k = \mathbf H\mathbf x_k + \mathbf v. \]

Typical assumptions include

\[ E[\mathbf w_k]=0, \qquad E[\mathbf v_k]=0, \]

with known covariance matrices \(Q\) and \(R\).

The basic formulation usually assumes process noise and measurement noise are mutually uncorrelated.

Gaussian assumptions are especially important when the KF is interpreted as representing the complete posterior distribution.

34. What Happens When the System Is Nonlinear?

Real spacecraft systems are often nonlinear.

Orbital dynamics may be

\[ \dot{\mathbf x} = f(\mathbf x,\mathbf u). \]

A camera bearing measurement may be

\[ \alpha = \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{y}{x} \right). \]

LiDAR range may be

\[ \rho = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}. \]

Quaternion attitude kinematics are also nonlinear.

This motivates nonlinear Kalman-filter variants such as

\[ \boxed{\text{EKF}}, \qquad \boxed{\text{ESKF}}, \qquad \boxed{\text{MEKF}}, \qquad \boxed{\text{UKF}}. \]

Their implementations differ, but the fundamental prediction–innovation–correction architecture remains.

35. Filter Initialization

The filter requires an initial state estimate

\[ \boxed{\hat{\mathbf x}_0^+} \]

and an initial covariance

\[ \boxed{\mathbf P_0^+}. \]

Initial state information may come from:

  • GNSS,
  • ground-based orbit determination,
  • star tracker,
  • coarse Sun sensors,
  • a previous navigation solution,
  • target ephemeris,
  • relative-navigation initialization.

If \(P_0\) is unrealistically small, the filter may be overconfident and resist valid measurements.

If \(P_0\) is very large, the filter may respond aggressively to initial measurements.

36. Filter Convergence

The filter may require time to converge after initialization.

During convergence:

  • state errors should decrease,
  • covariance should become realistic,
  • innovations should become statistically reasonable,
  • Kalman gains may change significantly.

Convergence depends on:

  • observability,
  • measurement availability,
  • initial-state error,
  • initial covariance,
  • \(Q\),
  • \(R\),
  • sensor geometry,
  • dynamic excitation.

Running a filter longer cannot make fundamentally unobservable states observable.

37. Observability and the Kalman Filter

For

\[ x_{k+1}=Fx_k \]

and

\[ z_k=Hx_k, \]

the observability matrix is

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal O = \begin{bmatrix} H\\ HF\\ HF^2\\ \vdots\\ HF^{n-1} \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Full observability requires

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(\mathcal O)=n }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Filter} + \text{Measurements} \not\Rightarrow \text{All States Are Automatically Estimable} }. \]

38. Innovation as a Filter-Health Indicator

For a correctly modelled and statistically consistent filter, the innovation should behave approximately according to its predicted statistics.

Innovation behaviour can help identify:

  • incorrect \(Q\),
  • incorrect \(R\),
  • sensor bias,
  • model errors,
  • timing problems,
  • sensor faults,
  • outliers,
  • incorrect measurement models.

A persistent non-zero residual mean can indicate systematic error.

39. Normalized Innovation Squared — NIS

A common consistency statistic is

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{NIS}_k = \mathbf y_k^T \mathbf S_k^{-1} \mathbf y_k }. \]

Under the standard assumptions, NIS may be compared with chi-square bounds based on measurement dimension.

NIS can be used for:

  • measurement validation,
  • innovation gating,
  • consistency analysis,
  • Monte Carlo verification.

40. Normalized Estimation Error Squared — NEES

If truth is available,

\[ \mathbf e_k = \mathbf x_k-\hat{\mathbf x}_k. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{NEES}_k = \mathbf e_k^T \mathbf P_k^{-1} \mathbf e_k }. \]

NEES evaluates whether actual estimation error is consistent with the covariance predicted by the filter.

It is especially useful for:

  • simulation,
  • Monte Carlo analysis,
  • SIL testing,
  • HIL testing,
  • validation.

41. Filter Accuracy vs Filter Consistency

Two questions should always be considered separately:

\[ \boxed{\text{Is the state estimate accurate?}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{Is the stated uncertainty credible?}} \]

Useful validation metrics include:

  • RMSE,
  • mean estimation error,
  • standard deviation,
  • covariance envelopes,
  • innovation statistics,
  • NIS,
  • NEES,
  • convergence time,
  • maximum error.

42. Effect of Choosing \(R\) Too Small

If the assumed measurement covariance is smaller than the true sensor uncertainty,

\[ \boxed{ R_{\text{assumed}} < R_{\text{actual}} \Rightarrow \text{Overtrust Measurements} }. \]

Possible consequences include:

  • excessive measurement weighting,
  • noisy state estimates,
  • covariance becoming too small,
  • filter overconfidence,
  • higher sensitivity to outliers.

43. Effect of Choosing \(R\) Too Large

If \(R\) is too large, the filter under-trusts the sensor.

Consequences may include:

  • weak measurement corrections,
  • excessive reliance on prediction,
  • slow correction of model drift,
  • slower convergence.

44. Effect of Choosing \(Q\) Too Small

If \(Q\) is too small, the filter assumes that the dynamics model is more accurate than it actually is.

Possible consequences include:

  • poor response to real disturbances,
  • slow adaptation,
  • overconfidence,
  • underestimated covariance,
  • poor innovation statistics.

45. Effect of Choosing \(Q\) Too Large

If \(Q\) is excessively large, predicted covariance grows rapidly.

The filter then relies more strongly on measurements.

Possible consequences include:

  • noisier state estimates,
  • higher sensor-noise sensitivity,
  • unnecessary measurement-driven corrections.

\(Q\) and \(R\) should therefore be based on physical modelling and verified empirically.

46. Correlated Measurement Noise

Measurement covariance does not have to be diagonal.

For example,

\[ \mathbf R = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_x^2&R_{xy}\\ R_{yx}&\sigma_y^2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Non-zero off-diagonal terms represent correlation between measurement errors.

Ignoring significant correlations can cause the filter to count information incorrectly and become overconfident.

47. Sequential Multi-Sensor Updates

Measurements from GNSS, cameras, LiDAR, star trackers and other sensors need not arrive simultaneously.

The filter may execute:

\[ \boxed{\text{Prediction}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{GNSS Update}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Prediction}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Camera Update}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{LiDAR Update}}. \]

This naturally supports asynchronous spacecraft sensors.

48. Multi-Rate Sensor Fusion

Typical spacecraft sensor rates may differ significantly.

  • Gyroscope — high rate,
  • IMU — high rate,
  • Star tracker — lower rate,
  • GNSS — moderate rate,
  • Camera — mission-dependent,
  • LiDAR — mission-dependent.

A common architecture is

\[ \boxed{ \text{High-Rate Prediction} + \text{Asynchronous Measurement Updates} }. \]

49. Sensor Dropout

If a sensor measurement is unavailable, its update is skipped.

The filter continues propagation:

\[ \boxed{ \hat x^+ \rightarrow \text{Prediction} \rightarrow \hat x^- }. \]

State uncertainty generally grows because process noise continues to accumulate.

Once the sensor becomes valid again, measurement updates resume.

50. Measurement Gating

Not every received measurement should automatically be accepted.

For example, an RPO camera may incorrectly associate a visual feature.

A common validation chain is

\[ \boxed{\text{Measurement}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Innovation}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Statistical Gating}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Accept / Reject / Down-Weight}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Kalman Update}}. \]

NIS and Mahalanobis-distance tests can be used for this purpose.

51. Time Synchronization and Latency

A measurement must be associated with the correct state epoch.

Real spacecraft sensors may contain:

  • different timestamps,
  • processing delays,
  • communication latency,
  • buffering delays,
  • asynchronous clocks.

If a measurement generated at

\[ t-\tau \]

is incorrectly interpreted as belonging to \(t\), the innovation can contain timing error.

Therefore a practical measurement requires

\[ \boxed{ \text{Measurement Value} + \text{Measurement Covariance} + \text{Correct Timestamp} }. \]

52. Numerical Implementation Considerations

Practical implementation requires attention to:

  • covariance symmetry,
  • positive-semidefinite covariance,
  • matrix conditioning,
  • floating-point precision,
  • discretization accuracy,
  • update timing,
  • matrix dimensions,
  • sensor units,
  • coordinate frames.

Although the theoretical formula contains

\[ S^{-1}, \]

software implementations should generally solve the associated linear system rather than explicitly forming a matrix inverse.

53. Covariance Symmetry

The covariance theoretically satisfies

\[ \boxed{P=P^T}. \]

Finite-precision computations can introduce small asymmetries.

A common safeguard is

\[ \boxed{ P \leftarrow \frac12(P+P^T) }. \]

However, repeated covariance asymmetry may indicate a deeper implementation problem and should be investigated.

54. Positive-Semidefinite Covariance

A valid covariance matrix must satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf a^T P \mathbf a \ge0 } \]

for any vector \(\mathbf a\).

Negative covariance eigenvalues caused by numerical errors are physically invalid.

This motivates robust covariance-update methods and, in demanding applications, square-root filtering.

55. Kalman Filter and Coordinate Frames

Every state and measurement must have an explicit coordinate frame.

Examples include:

  • inertial frame,
  • ECEF frame,
  • LVLH/Hill frame,
  • spacecraft body frame,
  • sensor frame,
  • target frame.

Suppose a sensor reports

\[ \mathbf r^S \]

in the sensor frame while the estimator state uses

\[ \mathbf r^H \]

in a Hill frame.

The appropriate transformation must be included in the measurement model.

Incorrect frame handling can cause estimator failure even when all Kalman equations are mathematically correct.

56. Kalman Filtering for Spacecraft Orbit Navigation

A basic orbital state is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Measurements may come from:

  • GNSS,
  • ground tracking,
  • range,
  • range rate,
  • inter-satellite links.

For highly nonlinear orbital dynamics or nonlinear measurements, an EKF or another nonlinear estimator is usually required.

57. Kalman Filtering for Attitude Determination

Attitude estimation can combine:

  • gyroscope,
  • star tracker,
  • Sun sensor,
  • magnetometer.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{\text{Gyroscope}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{High-Rate Attitude Propagation}} \]

while

\[ \boxed{ \text{Star Tracker / Sun Sensor / Magnetometer} } \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Measurement Update}}. \]

Because quaternion kinematics and reference-vector measurements are nonlinear, spacecraft attitude determination commonly uses the MEKF or related nonlinear formulations.

58. Kalman Filtering for IMU/GNSS Navigation

A representative navigation state is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v\\ \boldsymbol\theta\\ \mathbf b_g\\ \mathbf b_a \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The IMU provides high-rate propagation.

Its errors accumulate because of:

  • white noise,
  • gyro bias,
  • accelerometer bias,
  • scale factor,
  • alignment errors.

GNSS supplies long-term position and velocity information.

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\text{IMU}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{High-Rate Propagation}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{GNSS}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Position/Velocity Correction}}. \]

59. Kalman Filtering for Rendezvous and Proximity Operations

A basic relative-navigation state is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x_{rel} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{rel}\\ \mathbf v_{rel} \end{bmatrix} }. \]

A more complete state may additionally include:

  • relative attitude,
  • relative angular rates,
  • sensor biases,
  • sensor calibration parameters.

Dynamic models may include:

  • Hill/Clohessy-Wiltshire equations,
  • Tschauner-Hempel dynamics,
  • nonlinear relative orbital dynamics,
  • numerical orbital propagation.

Relative measurements may come from:

  • relative GNSS,
  • camera,
  • LiDAR,
  • range sensors,
  • bearing sensors.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Relative Dynamics Prediction} + \text{Relative Sensor Updates} \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf x}_{rel} }. \]

60. RPO Measurement Examples

Relative GNSS

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf z_{GNSS} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{rel}\\ \mathbf v_{rel} \end{bmatrix} + \text{noise} }. \]

Camera Bearing

\[ \boxed{ \alpha = \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{y}{x} \right) } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \beta = \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{z} {\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \right) }. \]

LiDAR Range

\[ \boxed{ \rho = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2} }. \]

These measurement models are nonlinear, which motivates EKF or UKF methods.

However, the fundamental estimator structure remains

\[ \boxed{ \text{Predict} \rightarrow \text{Predict Measurement} \rightarrow \text{Innovation} \rightarrow \text{Gain} \rightarrow \text{Correct} }. \]

61. Sensor Handover During RPO

Different sensors may dominate at different relative ranges.

A representative architecture is

\[ \boxed{\text{Far Range: Relative GNSS}} \]

\[ \downarrow \]

\[ \boxed{\text{Mid Range: GNSS + Camera}} \]

\[ \downarrow \]

\[ \boxed{\text{Close Range: Camera + LiDAR}} \]

\[ \downarrow \]

\[ \boxed{\text{Capture: Precision Relative Pose}}. \]

The estimator should maintain continuity throughout these transitions.

This requires proper handling of:

  • sensor availability,
  • measurement covariance,
  • observability,
  • initialization,
  • mode-dependent measurement models,
  • outlier rejection.

62. State Estimate vs Capture Decision

Suppose capture requires

\[ \|\mathbf r_{rel}\| < r_{capture} \]

and

\[ \|\mathbf v_{rel}\| < v_{capture}. \]

Checking only

\[ \hat{\mathbf r}_{rel} \]

and

\[ \hat{\mathbf v}_{rel} \]

may not be sufficient.

The associated covariance should also be considered.

Therefore mission logic can use both

\[ \boxed{\text{Estimated Relative State}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{Navigation Confidence}}. \]

63. Kalman Filter vs Complementary Filter

A complementary filter uses designed frequency weighting or fixed gains.

A Kalman Filter uses propagated uncertainty.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Complementary Filter} \rightarrow \text{Frequency / Designed Weighting} } \]

while

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Filter} \rightarrow \text{Model- and Covariance-Based Weighting} }. \]

64. Kalman Filter vs Luenberger Observer

A Luenberger observer may be written as

\[ \hat x_{k+1} = F\hat x_k + L(z_k-H\hat x_k). \]

Its structure resembles the Kalman Filter.

The fundamental difference is how the correction gain is obtained.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Observer Gain} \rightarrow \text{Designed from Error Dynamics} } \]

while

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Gain} \rightarrow \text{Derived from Uncertainty} }. \]

65. Steady-State Kalman Filter

For a time-invariant system under suitable conditions,

\[ P_k \rightarrow P_\infty. \]

Consequently,

\[ K_k \rightarrow K_\infty. \]

The filter can then behave similarly to a fixed-gain observer.

This creates an important link between deterministic observer design and stochastic estimation.

66. The Riccati Equation

Kalman covariance recursion is closely related to the Riccati equation.

The covariance evolves according to a balance between:

\[ \boxed{\text{Dynamic Propagation}}, \]

\[ \boxed{\text{Process Uncertainty}}, \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{Measurement Information}}. \]

The Riccati equation also appears in optimal-control theory, particularly LQR.

67. Duality with LQR

There is a mathematical duality between Kalman filtering and the Linear Quadratic Regulator.

  • LQR determines how control inputs should be selected to regulate a dynamic state.
  • Kalman filtering determines how measurements should be used to reconstruct an uncertain state.

Together,

\[ \boxed{\text{Kalman Filter}} + \boxed{\text{LQR Controller}} \]

form the classical LQG estimator-controller framework under appropriate assumptions.

68. Kalman Filter Does Not Remove Sensor Bias Automatically

A common misconception is that a Kalman Filter automatically removes sensor bias.

Bias must generally be represented in the model and be observable.

For example, gyro bias can be included as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \cdots\\ \mathbf b_g \end{bmatrix} }. \]

A simple bias evolution model is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf b_{g,k+1} = \mathbf b_{g,k} + \mathbf w_{b,k} }. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Unknown Error} + \text{No State/Error Model} \not\Rightarrow \text{Automatic Kalman Correction} }. \]

69. Kalman Filter Does Not Fix a Wrong Model

A sophisticated estimator cannot compensate indefinitely for incorrect physics or incorrect implementation.

Serious problems can arise from:

  • incorrect frame transformations,
  • wrong signs,
  • incorrect units,
  • incorrect timestamps,
  • wrong measurement functions,
  • missing sensor biases,
  • incorrect process models,
  • incorrect noise models.

Therefore estimator development must begin with

\[ \boxed{ \text{Physics} + \text{Frames} + \text{Units} + \text{Timing} + \text{Sensor Models} }. \]

70. Filter Divergence

Filter divergence occurs when the estimated state increasingly departs from truth or when the covariance no longer represents actual error.

Possible causes include:

  • incorrect \(Q\),
  • incorrect \(R\),
  • poor initialization,
  • strong nonlinearities,
  • unmodelled bias,
  • incorrect measurement association,
  • numerical instability,
  • timing error,
  • sensor fault,
  • poor observability.

Innovation monitoring and covariance monitoring are therefore essential for estimator health assessment.

71. Kalman Filter Verification

A Kalman Filter should not be validated using only one nominal simulation.

A verification campaign should include:

  • nominal runs,
  • measurement-noise variation,
  • process-model uncertainty,
  • initial-state error,
  • initial-covariance error,
  • sensor bias,
  • sensor dropout,
  • outliers,
  • timing errors,
  • sensor misalignment,
  • actuator uncertainty where relevant,
  • different trajectories and sensor geometries.

72. Monte Carlo Validation

Across many Monte Carlo runs, compare

\[ \hat{\mathbf x}(t) \]

with simulation truth

\[ \mathbf x(t). \]

Evaluate, where applicable:

  • position error,
  • velocity error,
  • attitude error,
  • bias error,
  • NIS,
  • NEES.

The purpose is not merely to obtain a smooth trajectory.

The objective is to demonstrate robust estimator behaviour over the expected uncertainty envelope.

A practical estimator implementation can be divided into:

  • Propagation,
  • Measurement Preprocessing,
  • Measurement Prediction,
  • Innovation Calculation,
  • Validation / Gating,

  • Covariance Management,
  • Health Monitoring.

A modular estimator is easier to test, verify and debug than a single monolithic implementation.

76. What the Guidance and Control System Receives

Navigation should provide a coherent state estimate such as

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\mathbf r}\\ \hat{\mathbf v}\\ \hat{\mathbf q}\\ \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\\ \cdots \end{bmatrix} }. \]

It should also maintain

\[ \boxed{\mathbf P}. \]

Guidance then determines desired motion, and control compares that desired motion against the estimated spacecraft state.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Sensors} \rightarrow \text{Kalman Filter} \rightarrow \hat x,P \rightarrow \text{Guidance} \rightarrow \text{Control} \rightarrow \text{Actuators} }. \]

77. Key Equations at a Glance

1. State Prediction

\[ \boxed{ \hat x_k^- = F_{k-1}\hat x_{k-1}^+ + B_{k-1}u_{k-1} } \]

2. Covariance Prediction

\[ \boxed{ P_k^- = F_{k-1} P_{k-1}^+ F_{k-1}^T + Q_{k-1} } \]

3. Innovation

\[ \boxed{ y_k = z_k-H_k\hat x_k^- } \]

4. Innovation Covariance

\[ \boxed{ S_k = H_kP_k^-H_k^T+R_k } \]

5. Kalman Gain

\[ \boxed{ K_k = P_k^-H_k^TS_k^{-1} } \]

6. State Update

\[ \boxed{ \hat x_k^+ = \hat x_k^-+K_ky_k } \]

7. Covariance Update

\[ \boxed{ P_k^+ = (I-K_kH_k)P_k^- }. \]

78. The Entire Kalman Filter in One Conceptual Equation

The central idea can be remembered as

\[ \boxed{ \text{New Estimate} = \text{Model Prediction} + \text{Uncertainty-Weighted Measurement Correction} }. \]

However, the filter actually maintains two coupled recursive processes:

\[ \boxed{\text{State Evolution}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{Uncertainty Evolution}}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Kalman Filter} = \text{State Estimation} + \text{Uncertainty Estimation} }. \]

79. Key Takeaways

  • The Kalman Filter is a recursive model-based sensor-fusion algorithm for linear stochastic systems.
  • It combines dynamic prediction and sensor measurements according to their uncertainties.
  • Its central quantities are \[ \boxed{ \hat x,\;P,\;Q,\;R,\;y,\;S,\;K }. \]
  • \(Q\) describes process-model uncertainty.
  • \(R\) describes measurement uncertainty.
  • The innovation \[ y=z-H\hat x^- \] represents disagreement between prediction and observation.
  • The innovation covariance \[ S=HP^-H^T+R \] determines how significant that disagreement is.
  • The Kalman gain \[ K=P^-H^TS^{-1} \] determines how strongly the measurement corrects the state.
  • Valid measurements generally reduce uncertainty.
  • Prediction without measurement generally increases uncertainty.
  • A measurement may improve states that it does not directly measure because of state coupling and covariance.
  • Initial state and covariance must be realistic.
  • Correct frames, units, timestamps, process models, sensor models, \(Q\), and \(R\) are essential.
  • Innovation statistics, NIS, NEES, Monte Carlo analysis and covariance consistency should be used during validation.

Most importantly,

\[ \boxed{ \text{A Kalman Filter Is Not Merely a Noise-Smoothing Algorithm} }. \]

It is a recursive probabilistic state estimator that continually asks:

\[ \boxed{\text{What Is the Spacecraft State?}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{How Certain Are We About That State?}} \]

80. From the Kalman Filter to Nonlinear Spacecraft Estimation

The classical Kalman Filter established the architecture

\[ \boxed{ \text{Prediction} \rightarrow \text{Innovation} \rightarrow \text{Kalman Gain} \rightarrow \text{Correction} }. \]

However, many important spacecraft models are nonlinear:

\[ \dot x=f(x,u) \]

and

\[ z=h(x)+v. \]

Examples include:

  • nonlinear orbital dynamics,
  • quaternion attitude kinematics,
  • star-vector measurements,
  • camera bearing measurements,
  • LiDAR range measurements,
  • relative-pose measurements,
  • nonlinear IMU navigation.

The next question is therefore:

How can the Kalman-filter framework be retained when \(f(\cdot)\) and \(h(\cdot)\) are nonlinear?

The first major approach is to linearize the nonlinear process and measurement models around the current state estimate.

This leads to:

\[ \boxed{\textbf{4.2.7 Extended Kalman Filter — EKF}} \]

where the nonlinear functions are propagated directly while Jacobians are used to approximate the local state and measurement sensitivity.
