4.3.11 Orbit Determination Fundamentals

Orbit determination is the process of estimating the trajectory of a spacecraft from imperfect measurements and a mathematical model of its motion.

The unknown quantity is typically the spacecraft state at some chosen epoch,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_0\\ \mathbf v_0 \end{bmatrix} }\]

where

\[\mathbf r_0\]

is the position vector and

\[\mathbf v_0\]

is the velocity vector at the reference epoch.

The estimated state is not obtained from measurement geometry alone. It results from combining

\[\boxed{ \text{Dynamics} + \text{Measurements} + \text{Uncertainty Models} \rightarrow \text{Orbit Estimate} }\]

Orbit determination therefore sits at the intersection of orbital mechanics, numerical propagation, measurement modeling, estimation theory, covariance analysis, and sensor tracking.

It is used in:

  • launch and early-orbit operations,
  • routine spacecraft navigation,
  • precise orbit reconstruction,
  • maneuver planning,
  • formation flying,
  • rendezvous and proximity operations,
  • conjunction analysis,
  • GNSS-based navigation,
  • deep-space navigation,
  • autonomous onboard navigation,
  • post-flight trajectory reconstruction.

4.3.11.1 What Orbit Determination Actually Estimates

The most basic orbit-determination problem estimates

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v \end{bmatrix} }\]

at a selected epoch.

But practical orbit determination may estimate more than position and velocity.

An augmented OD state can include

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_a = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v\\ \boldsymbol\theta \end{bmatrix} }\]

where

\[\boldsymbol\theta\]

may contain parameters such as

  • ballistic coefficient,
  • drag coefficient,
  • solar-radiation-pressure coefficient,
  • sensor bias,
  • clock bias,
  • timing offset,
  • maneuver correction,
  • empirical acceleration,
  • calibration parameters.

Thus orbit determination is often both a trajectory-estimation problem and a parameter-estimation problem.

4.3.11.2 Reference Epoch

An orbit estimate must be associated with a specific time.

Let

\[t_0\]

be the reference epoch.

The state at that epoch is

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_0 = \mathbf x(t_0). }\]

Measurements may occur before and after t_0, but the estimated reference state provides the common state from which the trajectory can be propagated.

The choice of reference epoch matters numerically because covariance and correlation can depend strongly on where the state is defined.

4.3.11.3 Why Use a Reference-Epoch State?

Suppose measurements occur at

\[t_1,t_2,\ldots,t_N.\]

Instead of estimating an independent state at every observation time, one can estimate a single state

\[\mathbf x_0\]

and propagate it to each measurement epoch:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x(t_k) = \phi( t_k,t_0,\mathbf x_0 ) }\]

where

\[\phi\]

denotes the nonlinear orbit-propagation mapping.

This enforces dynamic consistency across the complete observation arc.

4.3.11.4 State at Measurement Epoch

If the estimated reference state is

\[\hat{\mathbf x}_0,\]

then the predicted state at measurement time t_k is

\[\boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} = \phi( t_k,t_0,\hat{\mathbf x}_0 ). }\]

The superscript “-” often indicates a predicted state before incorporating the measurement at that epoch.

This propagated state is then used to predict what the sensor should observe.

4.3.11.5 Orbit Dynamics

Spacecraft translational dynamics are fundamentally governed by

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v }\]

and

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf a( \mathbf r, \mathbf v, t, \boldsymbol\theta ). }\]

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf x} = f( \mathbf x, t, \boldsymbol\theta ) }\]

with

\[\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v \end{bmatrix}.\]

The quality of orbit determination depends strongly on how accurately the acceleration model represents the actual spacecraft environment.

4.3.11.6 Two-Body Force Model

The simplest orbital force model includes only central-body gravity:

\[\boxed{ \ddot{\mathbf r} = -\mu \frac{\mathbf r}{r^3} }\]

where

\[r=\|\mathbf r\|\]

and

\[\mu\]

is the gravitational parameter.

This model is useful for fundamental orbital mechanics but is generally insufficient for precise long-duration orbit determination.

4.3.11.7 General Force Model

A practical force model may be written

\[\boxed{ \ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf a_{\rm grav} + \mathbf a_{\rm drag} + \mathbf a_{\rm SRP} + \mathbf a_{\rm third-body} + \mathbf a_{\rm thrust} + \mathbf a_{\rm empirical} +\cdots }\]

The exact set of terms depends on

  • orbital regime,
  • required accuracy,
  • mission duration,
  • spacecraft properties,
  • available measurements.

The force model should be accurate enough to support the required navigation performance without adding unnecessary complexity.

4.3.11.8 Non-Spherical Gravity

A real planetary gravity field is not perfectly spherical.

The gravitational potential may include spherical harmonics:

\[\boxed{ U(r,\phi,\lambda) = \frac{\mu}{r} \left[ 1 + \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^n \sum_{m=0}^{n} \bar P_{nm}(\sin\phi) ( \bar C_{nm}\cos m\lambda + \bar S_{nm}\sin m\lambda ) \right]. }\]

This captures effects such as

  • oblateness,
  • tesseral harmonics,
  • sectoral harmonics,
  • higher-order gravity terms.

For many Earth-orbit applications, the J_2 perturbation is especially important.

4.3.11.9 Atmospheric Drag

For low-Earth orbit, drag may significantly affect orbit prediction.

A simplified drag model is

\[\boxed{ \mathbf a_D = -\frac12 \rho \frac{C_DA}{m} v_{\rm rel}^2 \hat{\mathbf v}_{\rm rel}. }\]

Drag depends on

  • atmospheric density,
  • relative velocity,
  • drag coefficient,
  • projected area,
  • spacecraft mass,
  • attitude.

Uncertainty in any of these quantities can degrade orbit propagation.

4.3.11.10 Solar Radiation Pressure

Solar radiation pressure can be represented approximately as

\[\boxed{ \mathbf a_{\rm SRP} = P_\odot C_R \frac{A}{m} \hat{\mathbf s} }\]

with additional geometry and shadowing factors as required.

Here,

\[C_R\]

is the radiation-pressure coefficient.

For higher-altitude spacecraft or long prediction arcs, SRP can become important.

4.3.11.11 Third-Body Gravity

The gravitational influence of another body can be written in differential form as

\[\boxed{ \mathbf a_3 = \mu_3 \left[ \frac{\mathbf r_3-\mathbf r} {\|\mathbf r_3-\mathbf r\|^3} - \frac{\mathbf r_3} {\|\mathbf r_3\|^3} \right]. }\]

Third-body gravity is important for

  • high Earth orbits,
  • lunar trajectories,
  • cislunar navigation,
  • interplanetary missions.

4.3.11.12 Maneuver and Thrust Modeling

When a spacecraft performs a maneuver, its dynamics change.

For an impulsive maneuver,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf v^+ = \mathbf v^- + \Delta\mathbf v. }\]

For finite-duration thrust,

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf a_{\rm natural} + \frac{\mathbf F(t)}{m(t)}. }\]

Orbit determination must model known maneuvers correctly. Otherwise, the estimator may interpret maneuver effects as state errors or dynamic-model errors.

4.3.11.13 Maneuver Uncertainty

The commanded maneuver is not always equal to the delivered maneuver.

Let

\[\Delta\mathbf v_{\rm actual} = \Delta\mathbf v_{\rm cmd} + \delta\Delta\mathbf v.\]

Then

\[\delta\Delta\mathbf v\]

introduces additional uncertainty.

This may be represented using

  • maneuver covariance,
  • estimated maneuver correction,
  • process noise,
  • explicit augmented states.

4.3.11.14 Empirical Accelerations

Sometimes a precise physical force model is unavailable.

Small empirical accelerations may be introduced:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf a_{\rm emp} = \begin{bmatrix} a_R\\ a_T\\ a_N \end{bmatrix} }\]

in a radial-transverse-normal frame or another convenient basis.

These terms can absorb unmodeled accelerations.

However, they should be used carefully because good residual fit does not necessarily imply physically correct force modeling.

4.3.11.15 Numerical Orbit Propagation

For realistic force models, the equations of motion generally cannot be solved analytically.

Therefore numerical integration is used:

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf x} = f(\mathbf x,t) }\]

with numerical methods such as

  • Runge-Kutta methods,
  • adaptive integration,
  • multistep methods,
  • high-order orbit propagators.

The integration tolerance should be significantly tighter than the desired orbit-estimation accuracy.

4.3.11.16 Propagation Error

Numerical propagation introduces its own error.

Therefore OD accuracy depends on both

\[\boxed{ \text{Physical Model Accuracy} }\]

and

\[\boxed{ \text{Numerical Integration Accuracy}. }\]

Using an excellent force model with a loose integration tolerance can still produce poor predictions.

Similarly, extremely tight integration cannot compensate for an incorrect force model.

4.3.11.17 State Transition Matrix in Orbit Determination

For a small perturbation in the reference state,

\[\delta\mathbf x_0,\]

the perturbation at time t is approximately

\[\boxed{ \delta\mathbf x(t) = \boldsymbol\Phi(t,t_0) \delta\mathbf x_0. }\]

The STM

\[\boldsymbol\Phi(t,t_0)\]

is essential for

  • covariance propagation,
  • batch orbit determination,
  • measurement sensitivity,
  • differential correction.

4.3.11.18 Variational Equations

The STM satisfies

\[\boxed{ \dot{\boldsymbol\Phi} = \mathbf A(t) \boldsymbol\Phi }\]

where

\[\boxed{ \mathbf A(t) = \frac{\partial f} {\partial\mathbf x} }\]

and

\[\boldsymbol\Phi(t_0,t_0)=\mathbf I.\]

These equations are propagated alongside the spacecraft state.

4.3.11.19 Parameter Sensitivity in Orbit Determination

If a dynamic parameter

\[\boldsymbol\theta\]

is estimated, define

\[\boxed{ \mathbf S_\theta(t) = \frac{\partial\mathbf x(t)} {\partial\boldsymbol\theta}. }\]

Its dynamics are

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf S}_\theta = \mathbf A\mathbf S_\theta + \frac{\partial f} {\partial\boldsymbol\theta}. }\]

These sensitivities connect parameter uncertainty to the predicted orbit.

4.3.11.20 Observation Arc

An observation arc is the time interval over which measurements are collected and used for orbit determination.

Suppose observations exist from

\[t_{\rm start}\]

to

\[t_{\rm end}.\]

Then the arc duration is

\[\boxed{ T_{\rm arc} = t_{\rm end} - t_{\rm start}. }\]

The choice of arc length has a major effect on orbit-determination performance.

4.3.11.21 Short Observation Arcs

A short observation arc can provide quick updates but may have weak observability.

For example, position may be well constrained while velocity or dynamic parameters remain poorly determined.

Short arcs may also make it difficult to separate

  • initial-state error,
  • bias,
  • dynamic-model error,
  • slowly varying parameters.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Short Arc} \rightarrow \text{Potentially Weak Dynamic Information} }\]

4.3.11.22 Long Observation Arcs

Longer arcs allow dynamic effects to accumulate.

This can improve estimation of

  • velocity,
  • drag parameters,
  • biases,
  • maneuver corrections,
  • other slow parameters.

However, long arcs also expose the estimator to greater model mismatch.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Longer Arc} \rightarrow \text{More Information} + \text{More Sensitivity to Model Error} }\]

4.3.11.23 Arc-Length Tradeoff

The choice of observation arc is therefore a balance:

\[\boxed{ \text{Arc Length} = \text{Observability} \leftrightarrow \text{Model Validity} }\]

There is no universally optimal arc duration.

It depends on

  • measurement type,
  • orbit regime,
  • force-model quality,
  • parameter set,
  • maneuver activity,
  • required prediction interval.

4.3.11.24 Measurement Times

Measurements occur at discrete times

\[t_k.\]

At each time,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf z_k = h_k( \mathbf x(t_k) ) + \mathbf v_k. }\]

Orbit determination therefore requires accurate time tagging.

Timing errors can map directly into apparent position or velocity errors.

4.3.11.25 Measurement Prediction

Given the propagated state

\[\hat{\mathbf x}_k^-,\]

the predicted measurement is

\[\boxed{ \hat{\mathbf z}_k = h_k( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^- ). }\]

The difference between actual and predicted measurement is the residual or innovation:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf z_k - \hat{\mathbf z}_k. }\]

Residuals are central to orbit determination.

4.3.11.26 What Residuals Mean

The residual

\[\mathbf y_k\]

contains information about disagreement between measurement and prediction.

This disagreement can arise from

  • state error,
  • measurement noise,
  • sensor bias,
  • force-model error,
  • timing error,
  • calibration error,
  • maneuver error.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Residual} \neq \text{Pure State Error} }\]

Residual interpretation requires understanding the complete estimation model.

4.3.11.27 Measurement Noise

Measurements are generally modeled as

\[\boxed{ \mathbf v_k \sim \mathcal N( \mathbf0, \mathbf R_k ). }\]

The matrix

\[\mathbf R_k\]

describes measurement uncertainty.

Different measurements may have different noise levels and correlations.

The weighting used in OD should therefore reflect the statistical quality of each measurement type.

4.3.11.28 Measurement Weighting

For a scalar measurement with variance

\[\sigma^2,\]

the weight is commonly proportional to

\[\boxed{ w = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}. }\]

For vector measurements,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf W = \mathbf R^{-1}. }\]

Higher-quality measurements receive greater statistical influence.

However, incorrect weighting can make the solution inconsistent.

4.3.11.29 Measurement Geometry

Orbit determination quality depends strongly on geometry.

Two very accurate measurements may provide little new information if they constrain nearly the same state direction.

By contrast, measurements from diverse viewing geometries can significantly improve observability.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{OD Accuracy} \neq \text{Sensor Accuracy Alone} }\]

but rather

\[\boxed{ \text{OD Accuracy} = f( \text{Noise}, \text{Geometry}, \text{Dynamics}, \text{Arc Length} ). }\]

4.3.11.30 Measurement Partial Derivatives

Linearizing the measurement model gives

\[\delta\mathbf z_k \approx \mathbf H_k \delta\mathbf x_k\]

where

\[\boxed{ \mathbf H_k = \frac{\partial h_k} {\partial\mathbf x_k}. }\]

If the estimated variable is the reference-epoch state,

\[\delta\mathbf x_k = \boldsymbol\Phi(t_k,t_0) \delta\mathbf x_0.\]

Therefore,

\[\boxed{ \delta\mathbf z_k \approx \mathbf H_k \boldsymbol\Phi(t_k,t_0) \delta\mathbf x_0. }\]

This is the basis of the design matrix used in batch orbit determination.

4.3.11.31 Reference-Epoch Measurement Sensitivity

Define

\[\boxed{ \mathbf G_k = \mathbf H_k \boldsymbol\Phi(t_k,t_0). }\]

Then

\[\boxed{ \delta\mathbf z_k \approx \mathbf G_k \delta\mathbf x_0. }\]

The matrix

\[\mathbf G_k\]

describes how a perturbation in the reference-epoch state affects the measurement at time t_k.

4.3.11.32 General Orbit-Determination Architecture

The overall architecture can be represented as

\[\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Initial / Reference State Guess}\\ \hat{\mathbf x}_0 \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Orbit Propagation} \\ \hat{\mathbf x}(t_k) \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Measurement Prediction} \\ \hat{\mathbf z}_k = h(\hat{\mathbf x}(t_k)) \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Residual} \\ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf z_k-\hat{\mathbf z}_k \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Estimation Algorithm} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{State / Parameter Correction} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Updated Orbit Estimate} \end{array} }\]

This same basic architecture appears in both batch and sequential orbit determination.

4.3.11.33 Batch Orbit Determination

Batch OD processes a set of measurements over an observation arc simultaneously.

The unknown reference state is adjusted to minimize the weighted residuals.

Conceptually,

\[\boxed{ \{\mathbf z_1,\ldots,\mathbf z_N\} \rightarrow \text{Single Best-Fit Reference State} }\]

Batch OD will be treated in detail in Section 4.3.13.

4.3.11.34 Sequential Orbit Determination

Sequential OD updates the state as measurements arrive.

Conceptually,

\[\boxed{ \hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1|k-1} \rightarrow \text{Propagation} \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf x}_{k|k-1} \rightarrow \text{Measurement Update} \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf x}_{k|k}. }\]

This is the foundation of real-time recursive orbit determination.

Sequential OD will be treated in Section 4.3.14.

4.3.11.35 Batch versus Sequential OD

Batch and sequential OD use the same physical ingredients:

  • dynamics,
  • measurements,
  • uncertainty models,
  • sensitivities.

The difference is mainly how measurement information is processed.

Batch OD:

  • processes an observation arc together,
  • is useful for post-processing,
  • supports high-accuracy reconstruction,
  • naturally handles smoothing over an arc.

Sequential OD:

  • processes data recursively,
  • supports onboard or real-time navigation,
  • updates continuously,
  • is suitable for autonomous operations.

4.3.11.36 Prior Information

Orbit determination may begin with an a priori state:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_0 \sim \mathcal N( \bar{\mathbf x}_0, \mathbf P_0 ). }\]

This may come from

  • previous OD solution,
  • GNSS navigation solution,
  • launch injection estimate,
  • tracking network solution,
  • propagated ephemeris.

The prior provides both an initial state and an uncertainty level.

4.3.11.37 Initial Guess versus Prior

An initial guess is simply the starting numerical value used by the estimator.

A prior additionally includes statistical confidence.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Initial Guess} \neq \text{Probabilistic Prior} }\]

unless uncertainty is explicitly assigned.

This distinction matters especially in batch least-squares estimation.

4.3.11.38 A Priori Orbit Covariance

The covariance

\[\mathbf P_0\]

describes uncertainty in the initial or reference state.

A small covariance gives strong prior confidence.

A large covariance gives measurements greater influence.

Therefore,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf P_0 = \text{Quantitative Statement of Initial Orbit Knowledge} }\]

4.3.11.39 Orbit Covariance Propagation

If the covariance at t_0 is

\[\mathbf P_0,\]

then for deterministic dynamics,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf P(t) = \boldsymbol\Phi(t,t_0) \mathbf P_0 \boldsymbol\Phi^T(t,t_0). }\]

With process noise,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf P_{k+1|k} = \mathbf F_k \mathbf P_{k|k} \mathbf F_k^T + \mathbf Q_k. }\]

Thus orbit determination estimates both the nominal trajectory and its uncertainty.

4.3.11.40 Process Noise in OD

Process noise represents imperfect knowledge of the dynamics.

Possible sources include

  • atmospheric density error,
  • solar-radiation-pressure uncertainty,
  • maneuver error,
  • empirical accelerations,
  • unresolved disturbances.

Too little process noise can make the estimator overconfident.

Too much can make the orbit estimate unnecessarily noisy.

4.3.11.41 Deterministic versus Stochastic OD

A deterministic OD model assumes all relevant dynamics are explicitly modeled.

A stochastic OD model allows unmodeled behavior through process noise or estimated stochastic parameters.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Deterministic Model} + \text{Stochastic Correction} }\]

is often a practical representation of real spacecraft dynamics.

4.3.11.42 Observability in Orbit Determination

An orbit can be estimated only if the measurement set provides sufficient information about the state.

For a linearized system, observability depends on

\[\mathbf H_k\]

and

\[\boldsymbol\Phi(t_k,t_0).\]

The accumulated information from multiple observations determines whether all state components are recoverable.

4.3.11.43 Weakly Observed Directions

Some state combinations may be poorly observed.

Examples can occur when

  • geometry changes very little,
  • all measurements are nearly collinear,
  • the observation arc is too short,
  • only one measurement type is available.

The corresponding covariance becomes large along weakly observed directions.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Weak Geometry} \rightarrow \text{Anisotropic Orbit Uncertainty} }\]

4.3.11.44 Position and Velocity Observability

A single position-like observation does not necessarily determine velocity well.

Velocity becomes observable through

  • repeated measurements,
  • dynamic propagation,
  • range-rate or Doppler,
  • changing measurement geometry.

Therefore orbital dynamics provide essential coupling between position and velocity estimation.

4.3.11.45 Bias versus State Observability

Suppose a range measurement contains an unknown bias:

\[\rho_m = \rho+b_\rho+n.\]

If b_\rho is estimated together with position, the two may become correlated.

Sufficient geometry or independent measurement information is needed to separate them.

This is a general example of augmented-state observability.

4.3.11.46 Measurement Types in Orbit Determination

Common OD measurements include

  • range,
  • range rate,
  • Doppler,
  • azimuth,
  • elevation,
  • right ascension,
  • declination,
  • line of sight,
  • GNSS pseudorange,
  • GNSS carrier phase,
  • optical angle measurements,
  • inter-satellite range,
  • inter-satellite range rate.

Each measurement constrains the orbit differently.

Their detailed mathematical models are treated in Section 4.3.12.

4.3.11.47 Range Measurements

Range provides geometric distance between observer and spacecraft:

\[\boxed{ \rho = \| \mathbf r_{\rm sc} - \mathbf r_{\rm obs} \|. }\]

Range strongly constrains the radial separation along the line of sight but may provide weaker information in transverse directions by itself.

4.3.11.48 Range-Rate Measurements

Range rate is approximately

\[\boxed{ \dot{\rho} = \frac{ (\mathbf r_{\rm sc}-\mathbf r_{\rm obs})^T (\mathbf v_{\rm sc}-\mathbf v_{\rm obs}) }{ \rho }. }\]

It directly constrains relative velocity along the line of sight.

Range and range rate therefore provide complementary information.

4.3.11.49 Angular Measurements

Angles such as azimuth/elevation or RA/Dec constrain line-of-sight direction.

They can provide excellent angular geometry but may contain little direct range information.

Thus angle-only OD often relies strongly on

  • orbital dynamics,
  • changing observer geometry,
  • sufficiently long observation arcs.

4.3.11.50 GNSS Orbit Determination

A spacecraft equipped with GNSS can use satellite measurements to estimate position and velocity.

A navigation state may include

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v\\ b_c\\ \dot b_c \end{bmatrix} }\]

where

\[b_c\]

is clock bias.

GNSS can support both direct navigation solutions and higher-accuracy filtered orbit determination.

4.3.11.51 Optical Orbit Determination

Optical observations commonly provide angular measurements.

The measurement can be represented by a line-of-sight unit vector:

\[\boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol\rho} = \frac{ \mathbf r_{\rm target} - \mathbf r_{\rm observer} }{ \| \mathbf r_{\rm target} - \mathbf r_{\rm observer} \| }. }\]

Optical OD therefore depends strongly on geometry and dynamic propagation.

4.3.11.52 Relative Orbit Determination

In relative navigation, the state may be

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_{\rm rel} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{\rm rel}\\ \mathbf v_{\rm rel} \end{bmatrix}. }\]

Measurements may include

  • relative range,
  • relative range rate,
  • line of sight,
  • image-based bearings,
  • inter-satellite GNSS,
  • LiDAR,
  • radar.

The same OD principles apply:

\[\boxed{ \text{Relative Dynamics} + \text{Relative Measurements} \rightarrow \text{Relative Orbit Estimate}. }\]

This is a general principle for formation flying and rendezvous/proximity operations.

4.3.11.53 Absolute versus Relative OD

Absolute OD estimates motion relative to an inertial or Earth-fixed reference frame.

Relative OD estimates motion of one object with respect to another.

These problems may use different dynamics and measurements, but the estimation structure is the same:

\[\boxed{ \text{State Propagation} + \text{Measurement Prediction} + \text{Residual Processing} }\]

4.3.11.54 Tracking Stations

Ground-based OD may use measurements from multiple tracking stations.

Each station has known or estimated location

\[\mathbf r_{\rm station}(t).\]

Station geometry changes as Earth rotates.

Multiple stations improve geometric diversity and can improve orbit observability.

4.3.11.55 Tracking-Station Errors

Ground station parameters can also contribute error:

  • station-position uncertainty,
  • timing bias,
  • tropospheric delay,
  • ionospheric delay,
  • antenna bias,
  • measurement calibration.

High-accuracy OD may need to model or estimate some of these effects.

4.3.11.56 Time Systems

Precise orbit determination depends on consistent time handling.

Relevant time systems may include

  • UTC,
  • TAI,
  • TT,
  • GPS time,
  • UT1.

Time-scale conversion errors can produce apparent measurement or propagation errors.

Therefore,

\[\boxed{ \text{Correct Time Reference} \text{ is part of the OD model.} }\]

4.3.11.57 Coordinate Frames

Orbit states and measurements may be expressed in different frames.

Common frames include

  • inertial frames,
  • Earth-fixed frames,
  • local orbital frames,
  • topocentric frames,
  • sensor frames.

The transformation between them must be handled consistently.

A frame error can appear as an orbit-estimation error.

4.3.11.58 Earth Orientation

High-precision Earth-orbit OD may require Earth orientation information such as

  • polar motion,
  • UT1,
  • precession,
  • nutation,
  • Earth rotation.

These quantities are required to transform accurately between inertial and Earth-fixed coordinates.

4.3.11.59 Measurement Light-Time

For high-accuracy tracking, electromagnetic signals do not propagate instantaneously.

The measurement may correspond to spacecraft state at transmission or reflection time rather than reception time.

Therefore a light-time equation may need to be solved.

Conceptually,

\[\boxed{ t_{\rm receive} - t_{\rm transmit} = \frac{\rho}{c}. }\]

Ignoring light time can introduce measurement-model error.

4.3.11.60 Relativistic Corrections

In high-precision applications, relativistic effects may need to be included in

  • signal propagation,
  • clock models,
  • orbital dynamics.

These are usually negligible for basic OD but important for precision GNSS, deep-space navigation, and high-accuracy tracking.

4.3.11.61 Measurement Availability

Measurements are not always continuous.

Tracking may contain

  • visibility gaps,
  • occultations,
  • ground-station outages,
  • sensor duty cycles,
  • communication gaps.

During these periods, covariance typically grows through propagation.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{No Measurement} \rightarrow \text{Increasing Dependence on Dynamics}. }\]

4.3.11.62 Measurement Cadence

Higher measurement rate does not automatically mean proportionally better orbit accuracy.

Improvement depends on

  • measurement independence,
  • sensor noise,
  • geometry,
  • dynamic evolution,
  • correlation.

Repeated nearly identical measurements may add less information than fewer measurements with diverse geometry.

4.3.11.63 Measurement Correlation

Measurement errors may be correlated in time or across sensors.

If correlated measurements are treated as independent, the estimator may become overconfident.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Correct Statistical Modeling} }\]

is as important as accurate measurement equations.

4.3.11.64 Measurement Editing

Real tracking data may contain outliers or invalid measurements.

Measurement editing may include

  • range checks,
  • residual checks,
  • innovation gating,
  • quality flags,
  • sensor-health checks.

Outlier rejection should be statistically justified so that valid but unusual measurements are not removed systematically.

4.3.11.65 Pre-Fit Residuals

Before updating the estimate, define

\[\boxed{ \mathbf r_k^{\rm pre} = \mathbf z_k - h( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{-} ). }\]

Pre-fit residuals indicate how well the predicted trajectory matches the measurements before correction.

Large structured residuals may indicate model problems.

4.3.11.66 Post-Fit Residuals

After estimation, evaluate

\[\boxed{ \mathbf r_k^{\rm post} = \mathbf z_k - h( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^{+} ). }\]

Post-fit residuals help assess

  • estimator quality,
  • remaining bias,
  • model adequacy,
  • measurement consistency.

Small residuals alone do not guarantee a physically correct solution.

4.3.11.67 Residual Whitening

For a well-modeled estimation problem, normalized residuals should be approximately

  • zero mean,
  • statistically consistent with assumed covariance,
  • temporally uncorrelated.

Persistent temporal structure may indicate unmodeled dynamics or biased measurements.

4.3.11.68 Solution Convergence

An OD solution is considered converged when further correction becomes sufficiently small and residual statistics stabilize.

Possible criteria include

\[\boxed{ \|\delta\mathbf x\| < \epsilon_x }\]

and

\[\boxed{ |J_{i+1}-J_i| < \epsilon_J. }\]

Convergence criteria should be tied to engineering accuracy rather than arbitrary numerical thresholds.

4.3.11.69 Convergence Does Not Guarantee Correctness

An estimator can converge to the wrong solution.

Possible causes include

  • poor initial guess,
  • local minimum,
  • wrong force model,
  • biased measurements,
  • poor observability,
  • incorrect weighting.

Therefore,

\[\boxed{ \text{Numerical Convergence} \neq \text{Physical Correctness} }\]

Independent validation is essential.

4.3.11.70 Orbit Solution Validation

An orbit solution should be checked using

  • residual statistics,
  • covariance consistency,
  • independent tracking data,
  • withheld measurements,
  • prediction performance,
  • comparison across arcs,
  • physical plausibility.

A good OD solution should not only fit current measurements but also predict future measurements reasonably well.

4.3.11.71 Overlap Analysis

Two OD arcs may overlap in time.

If both solutions are propagated into the overlap region, their difference provides a useful consistency diagnostic.

Conceptually,

\[\boxed{ \Delta\mathbf x_{\rm overlap} = \mathbf x_A - \mathbf x_B. }\]

Small overlap differences can support confidence in the OD process.

4.3.11.72 Prediction Error

Orbit determination and orbit prediction are related but different.

OD asks:

What trajectory best explains the measurements?

Prediction asks:

Where will the spacecraft be later?

Prediction accuracy depends strongly on force-model and parameter accuracy.

A highly accurate fitted orbit can still diverge during long propagation if important dynamic parameters are poorly modeled.

4.3.11.73 Fit Span versus Prediction Span

The fit span is the interval containing measurements used in OD.

The prediction span is the future interval over which the estimated orbit is propagated without new measurements.

These should not be confused.

A solution may fit the observation arc very well while having poor long-term predictive performance.

4.3.11.74 Smoothing and Orbit Reconstruction

After all measurements over an arc are available, smoothing can improve state estimates at earlier times.

Conceptually,

\[\boxed{ \text{Forward Filter} + \text{Future Measurements} \rightarrow \text{Smoothed Orbit}. }\]

This is valuable for precise post-flight reconstruction and scientific analysis.

4.3.11.75 Sequential Onboard OD

For autonomous spacecraft, OD may run onboard.

An onboard estimator may combine

  • GNSS,
  • optical measurements,
  • inertial sensors,
  • inter-satellite ranging,
  • propagated dynamics.

The resulting state can support autonomous guidance and control.

4.3.11.76 Onboard versus Ground OD

Ground OD typically has access to

  • greater computational resources,
  • larger tracking networks,
  • more detailed force models,
  • delayed but high-accuracy processing.

Onboard OD prioritizes

  • real-time availability,
  • autonomy,
  • limited computation,
  • robust operation.

Many missions use both.

4.3.11.77 Initial Orbit Determination

Before a refined estimator can operate, an initial orbit may need to be generated from limited observations.

This is called initial orbit determination.

Its purpose is generally to produce a sufficiently accurate initial state for later differential correction or filtering.

IOD methods depend strongly on the available measurement type.

4.3.11.78 Initial Orbit versus Precise Orbit

Initial orbit determination typically provides a coarse solution.

Precise OD iteratively or sequentially refines that solution using

  • higher-fidelity dynamics,
  • many measurements,
  • calibrated sensor models,
  • covariance weighting,
  • estimated parameters.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Initial Orbit} \rightarrow \text{Refined Orbit Determination} }\]

4.3.11.79 Dynamic Consistency

An essential feature of OD is that measurements at different times are linked through the equations of motion.

The estimate should therefore satisfy

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x(t_2) = \phi( t_2,t_1,\mathbf x(t_1) ) }\]

within the modeled uncertainty.

This is what differentiates orbit determination from simply fitting independent positions.

4.3.11.80 Data Arc Continuity

If a long data gap occurs, the orbit must be propagated through that gap.

Uncertainty grows according to dynamics and process noise.

When tracking resumes, the estimator must reconcile the new measurements with the propagated state.

Large gaps can therefore challenge convergence and data association.

4.3.11.81 Maneuver Handling within an Arc

If a maneuver occurs inside an observation arc, the propagator must account for it.

Options include

  • known impulsive \Delta v,
  • known finite burn,
  • estimated maneuver magnitude,
  • split arcs,
  • process-noise injection.

Ignoring a maneuver can create large systematic residuals.

4.3.11.82 Multiple Observation Arcs

Sometimes separate arcs are processed independently.

This can be useful for

  • assessing consistency,
  • detecting model drift,
  • estimating changing parameters,
  • validating long-term prediction.

Differences between arc solutions can reveal unmodeled behavior.

4.3.11.83 Multi-Sensor Orbit Determination

Multiple sensors can be combined:

\[\boxed{ \text{Range} + \text{Doppler} + \text{Angles} + \text{GNSS} +\cdots \rightarrow \text{Improved Orbit Knowledge}. }\]

The benefit is strongest when the sensors provide complementary information.

4.3.11.84 Sensor Bias in Multi-Sensor OD

Different sensors may contain different biases.

If these are not modeled, combining sensors can actually degrade the solution.

Possible augmented states include

\[b_{\rm range}, \quad b_{\rm Doppler}, \quad b_{\rm clock}, \quad b_{\rm angle}.\]

Calibration and observability should be assessed before adding these states.

4.3.11.85 Orbit Determination Error Budget

An OD error budget may include

  • initial-state uncertainty,
  • measurement noise,
  • measurement bias,
  • force-model uncertainty,
  • station-location error,
  • timing error,
  • maneuver uncertainty,
  • environmental uncertainty.

The final orbit uncertainty is produced by the propagation and interaction of all these sources.

4.3.11.86 Covariance Is Part of the Orbit Solution

An OD result should ideally be reported as

\[\boxed{ \{ \hat{\mathbf x}, \mathbf P \}. }\]

The state estimate tells where the spacecraft is believed to be.

The covariance describes uncertainty in that belief.

A state without uncertainty information is an incomplete navigation product.

4.3.11.87 State Accuracy versus State Precision

Accuracy refers to closeness to the true state.

Precision refers to dispersion or uncertainty.

A solution can be

  • precise but biased,
  • accurate but noisy,
  • both accurate and precise,
  • neither.

Therefore covariance alone is not enough to prove accuracy.

4.3.11.88 Orbit Determination Consistency

Estimator consistency asks whether actual errors match predicted covariance statistics.

When truth is available,

\[\boxed{ \epsilon_x = \tilde{\mathbf x}^T \mathbf P^{-1} \tilde{\mathbf x} }\]

can be used.

When truth is unavailable, innovation statistics provide indirect consistency checks.

4.3.11.89 Common Mistakes in Orbit Determination

Common OD errors include:

  1. Using an inadequate force model.
  2. Ignoring measurement bias.
  3. Mixing coordinate frames.
  4. Using inconsistent time systems.
  5. Using incorrect station coordinates.
  6. Ignoring maneuver uncertainty.
  7. Assuming measurement errors are independent when they are not.
  8. Using unrealistic measurement weights.
  9. Choosing an observation arc that is too short for observability.
  10. Choosing an excessively long arc with poor model fidelity.
  11. Ignoring parameter-state correlations.
  12. Reporting covariance without validating consistency.
  13. Assuming small residuals mean the physical model is correct.
  14. Confusing numerical convergence with physical correctness.
  15. Using a high-fidelity estimator with a low-fidelity measurement model.

4.3.11.90 Practical Orbit-Determination Workflow

A disciplined OD workflow is

\[\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Define Reference Epoch and State}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Select Coordinate Frame and Time System}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Define Force Model}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Define Measurement Models}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Define Measurement Covariance}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Define Initial State and Covariance}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Propagate Orbit}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Predict Measurements}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Compute Residuals}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Compute Sensitivities}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Estimate State / Parameters}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Update Covariance}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Inspect Residuals}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Check Convergence and Consistency}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Validate with Independent Data}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{Assess Prediction Performance} \end{array} }\]

4.3.11.91 General OD Information Flow

The complete information flow is

\[\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Reference State}\\ \hat{\mathbf x}_0 \\ + \\ \text{Dynamic Model} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Propagated Trajectory} \\ \hat{\mathbf x}(t) \\ + \\ \text{Sensor Geometry} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Predicted Measurements} \\ \hat{\mathbf z} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Residuals} \\ \mathbf z-\hat{\mathbf z} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Estimator} \\ \downarrow\\ \text{Updated State + Parameters + Covariance} \end{array} }\]

This is the core structure underlying virtually all orbit-determination systems.

4.3.11.92 Orbit Determination as an Inverse Problem

Orbit propagation solves the forward problem:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_0 \rightarrow \mathbf x(t) \rightarrow \mathbf z(t). }\]

Orbit determination solves the inverse problem:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf z(t) \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf x}_0. }\]

The measurements are known, while the state that generated them must be inferred.

This inverse nature explains why

  • observability,
  • geometry,
  • measurement noise,
  • prior knowledge

are so important.

4.3.11.93 Deterministic and Statistical Components

OD contains both deterministic and statistical elements.

The deterministic part includes

  • dynamics,
  • coordinate transformations,
  • measurement geometry.

The statistical part includes

  • covariance,
  • measurement noise,
  • process noise,
  • uncertainty propagation.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Orbit Determination} = \text{Orbital Mechanics} + \text{Estimation Theory}. }\]

4.3.11.94 Relationship to Covariance Analysis

Section 4.3.9 established how uncertainty propagates.

In OD, that machinery becomes directly operational.

The STM propagates orbit uncertainty:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf P(t) = \boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf P_0 \boldsymbol\Phi^T. }\]

Measurements then reduce uncertainty according to their geometry and noise.

Thus,

\[\boxed{ \text{Orbit Determination} = \text{Trajectory Estimation} + \text{Uncertainty Estimation}. }\]

4.3.11.95 Relationship to Augmented-State Estimation

Section 4.3.10 showed how uncertain parameters can be estimated.

OD frequently uses this concept.

A typical augmented orbit state may include

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_a = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v\\ B\\ \mathbf b\\ \boldsymbol\theta_{\rm dyn} \end{bmatrix}. }\]

Thus, precise orbit determination often means estimating both trajectory and model parameters.

4.3.11.96 Relationship to Measurement Models

The estimator cannot use raw tracking data without a measurement model.

The central relationship is

\[\boxed{ \mathbf z_k = h_k( \mathbf x_k ) + \mathbf v_k. }\]

Section 4.3.12 develops the specific models for

  • range,
  • range rate,
  • Doppler,
  • azimuth/elevation,
  • RA/Dec,
  • line of sight,
  • GNSS,
  • optical observations.

These models form the measurement layer of OD.

4.3.11.97 Relationship to Batch Orbit Determination

In batch OD, all measurements are assembled into one estimation problem.

The reference-state correction is found from the combined measurement sensitivities.

Conceptually,

\[\boxed{ \text{Observation Arc} + \text{STM} + \text{Measurement Partials} \rightarrow \text{Best-Fit Reference State}. }\]

This is developed in Section 4.3.13.

4.3.11.98 Relationship to Sequential Orbit Determination

In sequential OD, measurement information is incorporated recursively.

The state and covariance evolve continuously:

\[\boxed{ \text{Propagation} \rightarrow \text{Measurement Update} \rightarrow \text{Propagation} \rightarrow\cdots }\]

This is developed in Section 4.3.14.

4.3.11.99 Key Equations

Reference state

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_0\\ \mathbf v_0 \end{bmatrix} }\]

Dynamics

\[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf x} = f( \mathbf x, t, \boldsymbol\theta ) }\]

Orbit propagation

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x(t_k) = \phi( t_k,t_0,\mathbf x_0 ) }\]

Measurement model

\[\boxed{ \mathbf z_k = h_k( \mathbf x_k ) + \mathbf v_k }\]

Measurement prediction

\[\boxed{ \hat{\mathbf z}_k = h_k( \hat{\mathbf x}_k^- ) }\]

Residual

\[\boxed{ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf z_k - \hat{\mathbf z}_k }\]

State transition mapping

\[\boxed{ \delta\mathbf x_k = \boldsymbol\Phi(t_k,t_0) \delta\mathbf x_0 }\]

Measurement sensitivity

\[\boxed{ \mathbf G_k = \mathbf H_k \boldsymbol\Phi(t_k,t_0) }\]

Covariance propagation

\[\boxed{ \mathbf P(t) = \boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf P_0 \boldsymbol\Phi^T }\]

or with process noise,

\[\boxed{ \mathbf P_{k+1|k} = \mathbf F_k \mathbf P_{k|k} \mathbf F_k^T + \mathbf Q_k. }\]

4.3.11.100 Key Takeaways

Orbit determination is the process of inferring a spacecraft trajectory from observations and a model of spacecraft motion.

The estimate is usually defined at a reference epoch:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_0\\ \mathbf v_0 \end{bmatrix}. }\]

That state is propagated to measurement times using the dynamic model:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf x(t_k) = \phi( t_k,t_0,\mathbf x_0 ). }\]

The propagated state is converted into a predicted measurement:

\[\boxed{ \hat{\mathbf z}_k = h( \hat{\mathbf x}_k ). }\]

The estimator uses the difference

\[\boxed{ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf z_k-\hat{\mathbf z}_k }\]

to improve the orbit estimate.

The quality of the solution depends jointly on

\[\boxed{ \text{Force Models} + \text{Measurement Models} + \text{Tracking Geometry} + \text{Observation Arc} + \text{Noise Models} + \text{Estimator Design}. }\]

The reference-epoch state, STM, covariance, measurement partial derivatives, and residuals form the mathematical backbone of orbit determination.

The observation arc must be long enough to provide information, but not so long that model errors dominate.

Measurements constrain different parts of the orbit differently, so diverse geometry and complementary sensor types are extremely valuable.

Orbit determination should always produce not only an estimated state but also uncertainty:

\[\boxed{ \text{OD Solution} = \{ \hat{\mathbf x}, \mathbf P \}. }\]

Finally, orbit determination is best viewed as a complete estimation architecture:

\[\boxed{ \text{Reference State} \rightarrow \text{Propagation} \rightarrow \text{Measurement Prediction} \rightarrow \text{Residual} \rightarrow \text{State Correction} \rightarrow \text{Updated Orbit and Covariance}. }\]

That architecture provides the foundation for the next topics: detailed orbit-determination measurement models, batch OD, and sequential OD.
